Thyroid & Parathyroid Surgical Anatomy

Thyroid surgery is an exercise in protecting four things: the recurrent laryngeal nerve, the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve, the parathyroid glands and their blood supply, and the airway. Know where each one sits relative to the gland before you hold a retractor.

The Thyroid Gland

Gross Anatomy

• Two lobes joined by an isthmus overlying tracheal rings 2–4

• Lobes extend from the oblique line of the thyroid cartilage down to about the 5th–6th tracheal ring

• Pyramidal lobe (thyroglossal remnant) in roughly half of patients, arising from the isthmus or adjacent lobe

• Normal weight ~15–25 g. Moves with swallowing because it is enclosed by pretracheal fascia attached to the larynx

• Ligament of Berry (posterior suspensory ligament) tethers each lobe to the cricoid and upper tracheal rings. The RLN lies immediately deep to it, or may pierce it

• Tubercle of Zuckerkandl: posterolateral extension of the lateral lobe. The RLN lies deep and medial to it, so it is a useful pointer to the nerve

Layers & Relations

What You Cut Through, and What Lies Behind

Superficial → deep: skin → platysma → investing fascia → strap muscles (sternohyoid, sternothyroid) → pretracheal fascia (false capsule) → true capsule → gland

Anterior: sternohyoid, sternothyroid, superior belly of omohyoid, overlapped by SCM

Posterolateral: carotid sheath, with the sympathetic chain behind it

Medial: larynx, trachea, pharynx, oesophagus, cricothyroid muscle with the EBSLN

Posterior: parathyroid glands and RLN in the tracheo-oesophageal groove

Clinical Pearl

The true capsule is adherent to the gland. Dissecting on it (capsular dissection) keeps the parathyroids and their end-arteries on the neck side of the plane.

Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve (RLN)

Course, Relations, Variants

Left RLN: arises from the vagus at the aortic arch, loops under it lateral to the ligamentum arteriosum, then ascends in the tracheo-oesophageal groove. More vertical and predictable

Right RLN: arises from the vagus at the subclavian artery, loops under it, then runs more obliquely upward and medially. Less predictable

Relation to the ITA: variable. The nerve may pass in front of, behind, or between branches of the artery. Never clamp blindly near the ITA

Entry to the larynx: behind the cricothyroid joint, deep to the lower border of the inferior constrictor. Supplies all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid

Non-recurrent laryngeal nerve: ~0.5–1% on the right, associated with an aberrant right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria). It runs directly from the vagus to the larynx. Very rare on the left

⚠️ Exam Trap

The right RLN loops around the subclavian artery; the left loops around the aortic arch (ligamentum arteriosum). The right non-recurrent nerve goes with an aberrant right subclavian, because the right fourth arch artery is absent and the nerve is no longer dragged down.

External Branch of the Superior Laryngeal Nerve (EBSLN)

The Forgotten Nerve at the Upper Pole

• Supplies the cricothyroid muscle (tenses the vocal cords, raises pitch)

• Runs on the inferior constrictor, close to the superior thyroid vessels, before piercing to supply cricothyroid

• Injury: loss of high pitch, voice fatigue. Disabling for singers and professional voice users

Cernea classification (relation to the upper pole):

  • Type 1: crosses the superior pole vessels >1 cm above the upper pole

  • Type 2a: crosses <1 cm above the upper pole

  • Type 2b: crosses below the upper pole. Highest risk

Mnemonic

Inferior = 3rd pouch, Superior = 4th pouch. The RLN separates the two: superior glands lie behind it, inferior glands in front of it.

Key Facts & Numbers

Landmark numbers, embryology and lymph node levels in one place.

Landmark Numbers

Must-Know

• Isthmus overlies tracheal rings 2–4

• Normal thyroid weight 15–25 g

• Parathyroid glands: usually 4; supernumerary (5th) gland in roughly 13%; normal gland ~30–50 mg

• Thyroidea ima present in ~3%

• Right non-recurrent laryngeal nerve ~0.5–1%

• EBSLN Cernea type 2b (below the upper pole) is the high-risk pattern

Embryology

Where Everything Comes From

Thyroid: endodermal downgrowth from the foramen caecum at the base of the tongue. Descends as the thyroglossal duct anterior to the hyoid. Remnants: thyroglossal cyst, pyramidal lobe, ectopic lingual thyroid

Parafollicular (C) cells: ultimobranchial body, which fuses with the thyroid. Source of calcitonin and medullary thyroid cancer

Superior parathyroids: 4th pouch. Inferior parathyroids: 3rd pouch, with the thymus

⚠️ Exam Trap

The inferior parathyroid comes from the 3rd pouch and the superior from the 4th. They swap order during descent, which is why the inferior gland is the one with the long, variable route and the more variable position.

⚠️ Exam Trap

Venous drainage is split. Superior and middle thyroid veins drain to the IJV; the inferior thyroid veins drain to the brachiocephalic veins. The inferior thyroid artery comes from the thyrocervical trunk, not the ECA.

Surgical Relevance

The operation in anatomical order: what you see, what you divide, and what you protect at each step.

Positioning and Exposure

Set-up to Strap Muscles

Position: supine, shoulder roll, neck extended, head on a ring. Head-up tilt (reverse Trendelenburg) reduces venous congestion. Eyes protected, arms tucked

Incision: collar incision in a skin crease, 1–2 fingerbreadths above the sternal notch, centred on the midline

Flaps: subplatysmal flaps up to the thyroid notch and down to the sternal notch

Midline: divide the linea alba between the strap muscles. Retract the strap muscles laterally. If a muscle must be divided for a large goitre, divide it high, because the ansa cervicalis supplies the strap muscles from below

Finding the RLN

Landmarks, in Order of Reliability

1. Tracheo-oesophageal groove, low in the neck, before the nerve crosses the ITA

2. Inferior thyroid artery: nerve may be anterior, posterior or between branches

3. Tubercle of Zuckerkandl: nerve lies deep and medial to it

4. Cricothyroid joint: follow the nerve upward to its entry point under the inferior constrictor

5. Not in the groove? Think non-recurrent nerve (right, aberrant subclavian). Check preoperative imaging for arteria lusoria

Intra-operative Neuromonitoring (IONM)

EMG endotracheal tube gives signal on nerve stimulation. Confirm signal before and after dissection. Loss of signal: stop, troubleshoot tube position and equipment, re-test. If loss is confirmed on the first side, consider staging the contralateral side to avoid bilateral palsy.

Parathyroid Preservation

Keep Them Alive

• Dissect on the thyroid capsule so the gland and its end-artery stay with the neck

• Ligate ITA branches at the gland, not the trunk

• Inspect every identified gland after the lobe is out. A dusky or black gland is devascularised

• Devascularised gland: confirm tissue (frozen section if unsure), mince into ~1 mm pieces, and autotransplant into a pocket in sternocleidomastoid. Mark the site with a non-absorbable suture or clip. Forearm muscle is used when future neck re-exploration is likely (e.g. MEN, renal disease)

• Inspect the specimen for an accidentally removed parathyroid

Airway: Neck Haematoma

Post-thyroidectomy Surgical Emergency

Bleeding under the strap muscles and pretracheal fascia compresses the airway and obstructs venous and lymphatic drainage, causing laryngeal oedema. It typically presents within the first hours, with neck swelling, pain, voice change or stridor.

Action: call for senior help, give oxygen, and open the wound at the bedside (remove skin sutures or clips and release the strap muscle layer) to decompress the neck. Then take to theatre for evacuation and haemostasis. Intubation can be very difficult because of laryngeal oedema; decompression comes first.

Special Anatomical Situations

What Changes the Plan

• Retrosternal goitre: most can be delivered by the cervical approach. Consider sternotomy or thoracic input for goitres arising in the mediastinum or with mediastinal blood supply

• Re-operative neck: scarred planes, displaced RLN, fewer identifiable parathyroids. Higher complication rates. IONM is particularly useful

• Large or invasive tumour: RLN may be encased. Decision to sacrifice depends on preoperative cord function and oncological need

• Parathyroid surgery: superior glands behind the nerve near the cricothyroid junction; inferior glands near the lower pole and thymus. Failure to find an inferior gland: explore the thyrothymic ligament and thymus. Covered fully in the parathyroidectomy guide

Which parathyroid is dorsal to the RLN, and what is its pouch of origin? flip ↓
The superior parathyroid lies dorsal (posterior) to the RLN and arises from the 4th pouch. The inferior parathyroid lies ventral to the nerve and arises from the 3rd pouch with the thymus.
What structure does each RLN loop around? flip ↓
Right RLN: right subclavian artery. Left RLN: aortic arch, lateral to the ligamentum arteriosum. The left nerve ascends more vertically in the tracheo-oesophageal groove; the right runs more obliquely.
What is a non-recurrent laryngeal nerve and when do you suspect it? flip ↓
A nerve that passes directly from the vagus to the larynx. ~0.5–1% on the right, associated with an aberrant right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria). Suspect it when the nerve cannot be found in the tracheo-oesophageal groove low in the neck.
Why is the tubercle of Zuckerkandl useful? flip ↓
It is a posterolateral extension of the lateral thyroid lobe. The RLN lies deep and medial to it, so it points to the nerve. Dissect carefully behind the tubercle.
Where does the RLN enter the larynx? flip ↓
Behind the cricothyroid joint, deep to the lower border of the inferior constrictor. It supplies all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid. The ligament of Berry is the high-risk spot just before this entry.
What does the EBSLN supply and what does injury cause? flip ↓
Cricothyroid muscle. Injury causes loss of high pitch and voice fatigue. Cernea type 2b (crossing below the upper pole) is the highest risk pattern. Prevent by ligating superior pole vessels individually close to the gland.
Where does the inferior thyroid artery arise, and how should it be ligated? flip ↓
From the thyrocervical trunk (branch of the subclavian). Ligate its branches at the thyroid capsule, not the main trunk, to preserve the parathyroid end-arteries. Its relation to the RLN is variable.
Venous drainage of the thyroid? flip ↓
Superior and middle thyroid veins drain to the IJV. Inferior thyroid veins drain to the brachiocephalic veins. The middle vein is short, wide and fragile, and is divided early.
A parathyroid gland looks dusky after lobectomy. What now? flip ↓
Devascularised. Confirm it is parathyroid tissue, mince into ~1 mm pieces and autotransplant into a muscle pocket (sternocleidomastoid), marking the site. Forearm muscle if future neck re-exploration is likely.
What are the clinical effects of unilateral versus bilateral RLN palsy? flip ↓
Unilateral: hoarse, breathy voice, weak cough, cord paramedian, airway usually adequate. Bilateral: stridor and airway obstruction because both cords sit in the paramedian position. This is an emergency.
Over which tracheal rings does the isthmus lie, and what is the thyroidea ima? flip ↓
Isthmus over rings 2–4. Thyroidea ima: inconstant (~3%) artery from the brachiocephalic trunk or aortic arch, ascending in front of the trachea. Bleeding risk in tracheostomy and low dissection.
First step for a post-thyroidectomy neck haematoma with stridor? flip ↓
Call for help, oxygen, and open the wound at the bedside (sutures or clips, then strap muscle layer) to decompress the neck, then return to theatre. Decompress before attempting intubation.

Question 1 of 5During upper pole dissection, a thyroid surgeon mass-ligates the superior thyroid pedicle high above the gland. The patient later reports voice fatigue and cannot reach high notes. Which structure was most likely injured?
A. Recurrent laryngeal nerve
B. External branch of the superior laryngeal nerve
C. Hypoglossal nerve
D. Ansa cervicalis
The EBSLN supplies cricothyroid, which tenses the vocal cords and raises pitch. It runs with the superior thyroid vessels, so mass ligation of the pedicle is the classic cause. RLN injury gives hoarseness and breathy voice, not isolated loss of pitch. Prevent EBSLN injury by ligating branches individually on the gland surface.
Question 2 of 5At thyroidectomy you identify a parathyroid gland lying posterior (dorsal) to the recurrent laryngeal nerve near the cricothyroid junction. Which statement about this gland is correct?
A. It is an inferior gland derived from the 3rd pouch
B. It is a superior gland derived from the 3rd pouch
C. It is a superior gland derived from the 4th pouch
D. It is an inferior gland derived from the 4th pouch
The RLN separates the glands: superior parathyroids lie dorsal to it, inferior parathyroids ventral. The superior glands arise from the 4th pharyngeal pouch and the inferior from the 3rd (they descend with the thymus). Superior glands have a fairly constant position; inferior glands are more variable.
Question 3 of 5During a right thyroid lobectomy you cannot find the RLN in the tracheo-oesophageal groove. A nerve runs transversely from the vagus directly to the larynx. This variant is most associated with which anomaly?
A. Thyroidea ima artery
B. Aberrant right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria)
C. Persistent thyroglossal duct
D. Retrosternal goitre
A right non-recurrent laryngeal nerve (~0.5–1%) occurs with an aberrant right subclavian artery. The right fourth arch artery is absent, so the nerve is not pulled down around the subclavian and passes directly from the vagus to the larynx. Suspect it when the nerve is not in the groove, and check preoperative imaging for arteria lusoria.
Question 4 of 5To preserve parathyroid blood supply during total thyroidectomy, how should the inferior thyroid artery be managed?
A. Ligate the main trunk near its origin from the thyrocervical trunk
B. Ligate the main trunk as it crosses behind the carotid sheath
C. Ligate its terminal branches on the thyroid capsule
D. Preserve it entirely and divide the upper pole only
Parathyroid glands depend on end-arteries from the inferior thyroid artery. Ligating the trunk devascularises them; ligating the terminal branches directly on the thyroid capsule keeps the parathyroid vessels on the neck side. Capsular dissection is also the safest plane relative to the RLN.
Question 5 of 5Four hours after total thyroidectomy a patient develops a tense, swollen neck and stridor. What is the most appropriate immediate action?
A. Urgent CT neck before any intervention
B. Open the wound at the bedside to decompress the neck, then go to theatre
C. Attempt intubation first and transfer to theatre
D. Give IV calcium gluconate for presumed hypocalcaemia
Neck haematoma after thyroidectomy is an airway emergency. Compression and laryngeal oedema can make intubation extremely difficult, so decompress first: call for help, give oxygen, remove skin sutures or clips and release the strap muscle layer at the bedside, then take the patient to theatre for evacuation and haemostasis. Hypocalcaemia causes tingling and tetany, not a tense neck swelling.
