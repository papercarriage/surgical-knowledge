Carotid & Cerebrovascular Anatomy

Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) is one of the most evidence-based operations in vascular surgery. Understanding the anatomy of the carotid bifurcation, the cranial nerves at risk, and the circle of Willis is essential before stepping into a CEA.

How to Distinguish ICA from ECA Intraoperatively

ICA vs ECA — 4 Distinguishing Features

1. ICA has NO branches in the neck — ECA has branches immediately

2. ICA is posterolateral at origin — ECA is anteromedial

3. ICA is larger at the bifurcation (carotid bulb)

4. Temporal tap test: tap the superficial temporal artery → transmitted pulsation felt in ECA (not ICA)

⚠ Exam Trap

The ICA has NO branches in the neck. If you see a branch, it's the ECA. This is a classic MCQ. The first ECA branch is the superior thyroid artery.

The Circle of Willis

Circle of Willis — Collateral Cerebral Circulation

Anterior circulation (ICA-derived):

ICA → Ophthalmic artery → Anterior choroidal → Posterior communicating (PCoA) → then divides into:

  • Anterior cerebral artery (ACA) — connected by anterior communicating (ACoA)

  • Middle cerebral artery (MCA) — main hemisphere supply; most common stroke territory

Posterior circulation (vertebrobasilar):

Vertebral arteries → Basilar artery → Posterior cerebral arteries (PCA) → connected to ICA via PCoA

Clinical Relevance

During CEA, cross-clamping the ICA temporarily occludes ipsilateral MCA territory. Collateral flow via ACoA (contralateral ICA) and PCoA (posterior circulation) determines tolerance. This guides shunting decisions — stump pressure <50 mmHg = shunt.

The Carotid Sheath

Contents — CCA, IJV, Vagus

The carotid sheath is a condensation of deep cervical fascia enclosing three structures:

• CCA — anteromedial

• IJV — posterolateral (and larger on the right)

• Vagus nerve (CN X) — posterior, between artery and vein

Medial to the sheath: thyroid, trachea, oesophagus, recurrent laryngeal nerve.

Posterior: sympathetic chain (injury → Horner's: ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis, enophthalmos).

Key Facts & Numbers

The numbers that come up in CEA indications, risk calculations, and MRCS MCQs.

Stenosis Measurement — NASCET vs ECST

Two Methods — Different Numbers for Same Stenosis

NASCET (North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial): stenosis % = (1 − minimal residual lumen / distal normal ICA) × 100

ECST (European Carotid Surgery Trial): stenosis % = (1 − minimal residual lumen / estimated original lumen at stenosis) × 100

ECST 80% ≈ NASCET 60% — ECST gives higher numbers. UK/international guidelines use NASCET.

⚠ Exam Trap

ECST and NASCET measure the same stenosis but give different percentages. An ECST 70% stenosis is only ~50% by NASCET. Always clarify which method. Current practice and all guidelines use NASCET.

Timing of CEA After Stroke/TIA

The Urgency Question

• After TIA or minor stroke: within 2 weeks (ideally within 48–72 hours if ABCD² score high)

• The highest stroke risk is in the first 72 hours after TIA — early CEA is protective

• After major stroke (disabling): wait 4–6 weeks (risk of haemorrhagic transformation)

• Crescendo TIA / stroke-in-evolution: urgent CEA within 24 hours

Clinical Pearl

"Time is brain" — the old practice of waiting 6 weeks after TIA before CEA is obsolete. NICE and ESVS recommend <2 weeks, and centres now aim for <48 hours in eligible patients.

Perioperative Stroke Risk

Acceptable Operative Risk Thresholds

CEA is only beneficial if operative stroke/death risk is below:

• Symptomatic patients: <6% (30-day stroke/death)

• Asymptomatic patients: <3%

High-risk features: contralateral ICA occlusion, tandem intracranial stenosis, cardiac comorbidity, recent stroke

Surgical Relevance

CEA anatomy step by step — what you'll see and do.

Carotid Endarterectomy — Operative Steps

Position, Incision, Exposure

Position: Supine, head ring, neck extended and rotated away, shoulder roll. Arms tucked.

Incision: Along anterior border of SCM from mastoid to just above clavicle. Centred on bifurcation (angle of jaw = approx. C3/4).

Dissection layers: Skin → platysma (preserve) → investing fascia → retract SCM laterally → IJV encountered → carotid sheath opened medial to IJV

Exposure sequence: CCA first (proximal control) → bifurcation → ECA → ICA (distal control beyond plaque). Heparinise before clamping.

Shunting Decision

When to Shunt — Protecting the Brain During Cross-Clamp

Routine shunting: Some centres shunt everyone — simplest approach.

Selective shunting based on:

• Stump pressure <50 mmHg (internal carotid back pressure after clamping)

• Awake monitoring (local anaesthetic CEA — patient speaks during clamping; neurological change = shunt)

• TCD/EEG changes under GA

On the Table

Local anaesthetic CEA ("awake CEA") is the gold standard for monitoring cerebral perfusion. The patient counting or answering questions provides real-time neurological feedback. Becoming more common in high-volume centres.

Arteriotomy & Endarterectomy

The Endarterectomy Plane

Arteriotomy: longitudinal on anterolateral wall of CCA, extended through bifurcation into ICA (avoiding ECA unless plaque extends there).

Plane: within the media — between inner and outer media layers. Plaque lifts as a cast. Use Watson-Cheyne dissector to develop plane circumferentially.

Distal endpoint: ICA intima must feather smoothly — if raised flap, place tacking sutures (6/0 Prolene) to prevent dissection.

Closure: Patch angioplasty (vein or Dacron/bovine pericardium) reduces restenosis risk and is preferred over primary closure in most guidelines.

Eversion CEA

Alternative Technique — Eversion Endarterectomy

ICA transected at origin → everted over itself → plaque removed from inside out → re-anastomosed.

Advantages: No patch needed; shorter clamp time; easier to lengthen kinked ICA.

Disadvantage: Harder to inspect distal endpoint; not suitable for high bifurcation.

Carotid Artery Stenting (CAS)

CEA vs CAS — When to Choose

CEA preferred: Symptomatic stenosis ≥70%, age <70, anatomically suitable (low bifurcation)

CAS preferred/considered: High surgical risk (contralateral RLN palsy, prior neck irradiation, hostile neck, severe cardiac disease), age >70 (peri-procedural stroke risk may be lower with CEA in elderly)

CREST trial: CAS associated with higher peri-procedural stroke; CEA higher MI risk. Overall equivalent long-term outcomes in low-risk patients.

What is the single most reliable way to distinguish ICA from ECA intraoperatively? flip ↓
The ICA has NO branches in the neck. If you identify a branch, it is the ECA. The first ECA branch is the superior thyroid artery.
What are the NASCET thresholds for CEA in symptomatic carotid disease? flip ↓
≥70%: strong benefit (NNT ~6). 50–69%: moderate benefit (especially men, recent symptoms). <50%: no benefit, medical management only. All using NASCET measurement.
Which cranial nerve crosses the ICA/ECA at the upper limit of CEA dissection and what happens if injured? flip ↓
Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII). Injury causes ipsilateral tongue deviation (towards the side of injury), dysarthria, and difficulty chewing. It is at risk during high dissection for a high bifurcation or long ICA plaque.
What stump pressure threshold indicates need for a carotid shunt? flip ↓
<50 mmHg internal carotid back-pressure after cross-clamping indicates inadequate collateral cerebral perfusion and the need for a shunt to maintain ipsilateral MCA territory perfusion during endarterectomy.
Why does carotid cross-clamping threaten the MCA territory specifically? flip ↓
The ICA divides into ACA and MCA intracranially. The MCA has no effective collateral supply from other territories — it is an "end artery" in functional terms. ACA has cross-filling via ACoA from contralateral ICA. MCA territory infarction causes contralateral hemiplegia and (dominant hemisphere) aphasia.
ECST 70% stenosis is approximately what by NASCET? flip ↓
ECST 70% ≈ NASCET 50%. ECST always gives higher numbers because it compares residual lumen to estimated original lumen (which is larger at the bulb). UK guidelines use NASCET — always clarify which method when reading imaging reports.
What is the timing recommendation for CEA after TIA? flip ↓
Within 2 weeks (ideally 48–72 hours) for TIA or minor stroke. Stroke risk is highest in the first 72 hours after TIA. Waiting 6 weeks (old practice) is now obsolete. Exception: major disabling stroke → wait 4–6 weeks to avoid haemorrhagic transformation.
What is the acceptable perioperative stroke/death rate for CEA in symptomatic patients? flip ↓
<6% (30-day stroke/death). Above this threshold, the operative risk exceeds the benefit of stroke prevention. For asymptomatic patients the threshold is <3% (lower because the background untreated stroke risk is lower).

Question 1 of 5A 72-year-old woman has a right-sided TIA 5 days ago. Duplex shows 75% right ICA stenosis (NASCET). She is medically optimised. When should CEA be performed?
A. Within 2 weeks
B. After 6 weeks of antiplatelet therapy
C. Only if she has a second TIA
D. Medical management only — age over 70 contraindicates CEA
Symptomatic stenosis ≥70% NASCET warrants CEA within 2 weeks (ideally 48–72 hours) per NICE and ESVS guidelines. Age alone does not contraindicate CEA. Waiting 6 weeks is the old approach and is now superseded — stroke risk is highest in the first 72 hours after TIA. Medical management alone is insufficient for high-grade symptomatic stenosis.
Question 2 of 5During a right CEA, after opening the carotid sheath, you identify the common carotid artery. You notice a small vessel arising from a structure posterolateral to the bifurcation. This vessel is most likely arising from:
A. Internal carotid artery
B. External carotid artery
C. Common carotid artery
D. Vertebral artery
The ICA has NO branches in the neck. Any branch arising near the bifurcation is therefore from the ECA. The ECA lies anteromedially at the bifurcation (the ICA is posterolateral). The first branch of the ECA is the superior thyroid artery. This is a classic CEA anatomy MCQ.
Question 3 of 5Following a left CEA, the patient has a hoarse voice. Indirect laryngoscopy shows a paralysed left vocal cord. Which nerve was most likely injured?
A. Vagus nerve (CN X)
B. Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII)
C. Ansa cervicalis
D. Marginal mandibular nerve
Vocal cord palsy after CEA = vagus nerve (CN X) injury. The vagus runs in the posterior aspect of the carotid sheath and its recurrent laryngeal branch supplies the intrinsic laryngeal muscles (except cricothyroid). CN XII (hypoglossal) injury causes tongue deviation, not hoarseness. Marginal mandibular injury causes lip droop. Ansa cervicalis injury causes strap muscle weakness (clinically minor).
Question 4 of 5An ECST report states 80% stenosis of the right ICA. What is the approximate NASCET equivalent?
A. 80%
B. 90%
C. ~65%
D. 50%
ECST gives higher percentage figures because it compares residual lumen to the estimated original diameter at the stenosis (which is larger at the carotid bulb). Approximate conversion: ECST 80% ≈ NASCET 65–70%. Current practice uses NASCET. An ECST 80% therefore corresponds to a NASCET stenosis that still warrants surgery in a symptomatic patient.
Question 5 of 5During awake CEA under local anaesthetic, you apply a cross-clamp to the ICA. The patient immediately reports left arm weakness and difficulty speaking. What is the most appropriate next step?
A. Convert to general anaesthetic and continue without shunt
B. Release the clamp immediately and insert a carotid shunt
C. Increase systemic blood pressure and observe
D. Proceed with endarterectomy quickly without shunting
Immediate neurological deficit after ICA cross-clamping in an awake patient indicates critical cerebral ischaemia from inadequate collateral flow. The clamp must be released immediately, and a carotid shunt inserted before re-clamping. This is the key advantage of awake CEA — real-time monitoring that triggers shunting before infarction occurs. Proceeding without a shunt risks major hemisphere stroke.
