Surface & Structural Anatomy
The building blocks that every breast cancer discussion, biopsy, and operation reference back to.

Surface anatomy

  • Extends vertically from the 2nd to 6th rib, and horizontally from the lateral sternal edge to the mid-axillary line.
  • The axillary tail of Spence projects from the upper outer quadrant into the axilla through an opening in the deep fascia — clinically important as breast tissue here can be mistaken for an axillary lymph node, and vice versa.
  • Divided clinically into four quadrants (upper outer, upper inner, lower outer, lower inner) plus a central (subareolar) zone — roughly half of all breast cancers arise in the upper outer quadrant, partly reflecting the greater volume of glandular tissue there.

The functional unit — TDLU

Terminal Duct Lobular Unit

The TDLU (terminal ductule + surrounding lobule) is where milk is produced and where the overwhelming majority of both benign and malignant breast pathology originates — invasive ductal and lobular carcinoma, DCIS, LCIS, and fibrocystic change all arise here, not from the larger lactiferous ducts themselves.

Layers, front to back

Skin → subcutaneous fat → glandular tissue & stroma (suspended by Cooper's ligaments) → retromammary space (a plane of loose connective tissue allowing breast mobility over the chest wall) → deep (pectoral) fascia → pectoralis major.

Cooper's ligaments

Why they matter clinically

These fibrous septa run from the deep fascia through the breast parenchyma to attach to the dermis, providing structural support. Tumour infiltration and subsequent contracture of these ligaments produces the classic clinical signs of skin dimpling and, when lymphatic obstruction is added, peau d'orange.

⚠️ Exam Trap

Nipple sensation is supplied predominantly by the lateral cutaneous branch of the 4th intercostal nerve (T4) — this is why nipple-sparing procedures carry a real risk of altered/lost nipple sensation, and why T4 dermatome is a favourite exam detail.

Vascular, Lymphatic & Nerve Supply
Know the split between medial and lateral supply — it explains everything from incision planning to metastatic spread patterns.

Arterial supply

SourceTerritory
Internal thoracic artery (perforating branches)Medial breast — the dominant supply, ~60%
Lateral thoracic arteryLateral breast
Thoracoacromial artery (pectoral branch)Deep/central breast
Posterior intercostal arteries (2nd–4th)Posterior/deep breast

Venous drainage & Batson's plexus

Venous drainage broadly mirrors the arterial supply (axillary, internal thoracic, and intercostal veins), but a clinically important secondary route exists via the vertebral venous plexus (Batson's plexus) — a valveless network connecting intercostal veins to the vertebral venous system. This allows breast cancer cells to reach the vertebrae and spinal cord without first passing through the lungs, explaining the pattern of vertebral metastasis sometimes seen without pulmonary involvement.

Lymphatic drainage

PathwayProportion
Axillary lymph nodes~75% (dominant pathway from all quadrants)
Internal mammary (parasternal) nodes~20% (especially medial/central lesions)
Other (interpectoral, supraclavicular, contralateral, intra-abdominal)Remainder

Cutaneous nerve supply

Anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves T4–T6 supply the overlying skin; the nipple-areolar complex is supplied predominantly by T4.

Surgical Relevance
Anatomy that directly informs incision planning, reconstruction, and clinical sign interpretation.

Oncoplastic planning

  • Incisions and flap design in oncoplastic surgery are planned around the known arterial territories — preserving the dominant medial (internal thoracic) supply is a priority when raising local tissue flaps.
  • The retromammary space is the surgical plane exploited during subcutaneous mastectomy and implant pocket creation — it allows the gland to be separated from the chest wall with minimal bleeding.

Clinical sign correlation

  • Skin dimpling: tumour tethering Cooper's ligaments — a hallmark sign prompting urgent triple assessment.
  • Peau d'orange: dermal lymphatic obstruction causing oedema around fixed hair follicles — associated with more advanced or inflammatory disease.
  • Nipple retraction: central tumour or duct shortening tethering the nipple-areolar complex.

Metastatic spread patterns

Beyond the lymphatic routes above, Batson's plexus explains why vertebral and pelvic bone metastases can occur via a purely venous route, bypassing the pulmonary capillary filter — relevant when interpreting a patient who has bone metastases without evident lung disease.

Viva pearl

If asked why nipple-sparing mastectomy carries a sensory risk, the expected anatomical answer is disruption of the T4 lateral cutaneous branch supplying the nipple-areolar complex — not a vague "nerves get cut" answer.
