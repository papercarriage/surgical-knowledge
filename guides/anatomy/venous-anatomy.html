Lower Limb Venous Anatomy

The venous system of the lower limb has three components: superficial, deep, and perforating. Understanding their anatomy underpins the management of varicose veins, DVT, chronic venous insufficiency, and the harvest of venous conduit for arterial bypass.

The Three Systems

Superficial, Deep & Perforating — The Triad

Superficial system: Above deep fascia. Great saphenous vein (GSV) and small saphenous vein (SSV). Valved. Drains via perforators and saphenofemoral/saphenopopliteal junctions into deep system.

Deep system: Below deep fascia. Paired veins accompanying arteries (venae comitantes). Join to form popliteal, femoral, iliac veins. Higher flow, higher pressure during exercise.

Perforating veins: Connect superficial to deep, piercing deep fascia. One-way valves direct blood deep-to-superficial at rest, deep-to-superficial during muscle contraction (in normal physiology — direction is always into the deep system). Incompetence → high-pressure backflow into superficial system.

⚠ Exam Trap

Blood flows from SUPERFICIAL to DEEP via perforators (not the other way). The calf muscle pump compresses deep veins during contraction, increasing pressure, which is transmitted superficially only if perforators are INCOMPETENT. Normal perforators allow flow superficial → deep only.

Great Saphenous Vein (GSV)

GSV — Course, Tributaries, Termination

Origin: Medial end of dorsal venous arch of foot

Passes: Anterior to medial malleolus → medial calf → medial knee (posterior to medial femoral condyle) → medial thigh → saphenous opening (fossa ovalis) → SFJ

Saphenofemoral junction (SFJ): 3.5 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle. GSV joins the common femoral vein (CFV) at the saphenous opening.

Tributaries at SFJ (ASEL mnemonic):

  • Anterolateral thigh vein (superficial circumflex iliac)

  • Superficial epigastric vein

  • External pudendal veins

  • Posteromedial thigh vein (accessory saphenous)

Clinical Pearl

In ~20% of patients the GSV is duplicated (especially in the thigh). Duplex mapping pre-operatively is essential — stripping the wrong vessel is a common surgical error.

Small Saphenous Vein (SSV)

SSV — Posterior Calf to Popliteal Fossa

Origin: Lateral end of dorsal venous arch → posterior to lateral malleolus

Course: Up the posterior midline of calf → pierces deep fascia of popliteal fossa

Termination: Saphenopopliteal junction (SPJ) — variable level (usually at the popliteal crease, but can be anywhere from mid-thigh to below knee). Must be localised by duplex pre-operatively.

Extension (Giacomini vein): SSV often communicates superiorly with GSV via the posteromedial thigh — the Giacomini/thigh extension vein. Relevant when SSV reflux propagates to thigh varices.

⚠ Exam Trap

The SPJ level is highly variable — the classic teaching of "knee crease" is only true in ~60%. Always duplex-mark before SSV surgery. Sural nerve (sensory to lateral foot/little toe) runs with the SSV — at risk during SSV stripping.

Important Perforating Veins

Named Perforators — Clinical Relevance

Cockett's perforators (I, II, III): Medial lower calf, posterior to tibia. The most clinically important. Incompetence → medial gaiter area ulceration (classic lipodermatosclerosis site).

Dodd's perforator: Mid-thigh, medial — connects GSV to femoral vein.

Boyd's perforator: Just below knee, medial — connects GSV to posterior tibial veins.

Hunterian perforator: Mid-thigh — connects GSV to femoral vein in Hunter's canal.

Key Facts & Classifications

CEAP, Trendelenburg test, DVT risk — the exam-ready facts.

C6Active venous ulcer
NICE Referral Criteria

Refer to vascular surgery: C2 with symptoms, or C3–C6. C4b, C5, C6 = fast-track referral. Duplex USS is mandatory before any intervention.

Trendelenburg Test

Two-Stage Test for Incompetence

Stage 1 (SFJ incompetence): Elevate leg to drain varices, compress SFJ with finger, ask patient to stand. If varices fill rapidly when finger released = SFJ incompetence (positive test).

Stage 2 (perforator incompetence): As above, but varices fill before releasing SFJ compression = perforators incompetent below the compression.

Modern practice: Duplex ultrasound has superseded the Trendelenburg test clinically, but understand the anatomy behind it for exams.

May-Thurner Syndrome

Left Iliac DVT — Anatomical Cause

The right common iliac artery crosses OVER the left common iliac vein at L5. This can cause chronic compression, leading to left-sided DVT and iliac vein stenosis.

Presents as left-sided DVT in young women, often triggered by oestrogen (OCP, pregnancy). Treatment: catheter-directed thrombolysis + iliac vein stenting.

DVT — Anatomy of Risk

Virchow's Triad & Venous Thrombosis Sites

Virchow's triad: Stasis + Endothelial injury + Hypercoagulability

Most common DVT site: Calf veins (tibial/peroneal venous sinuses)

Most dangerous: Iliofemoral DVT — highest PE risk, post-thrombotic syndrome

PE risk from DVT: Calf DVT ~1–2%; Proximal DVT ~40–50% if untreated

Surgical Relevance

GSV harvest, varicose vein surgery, and venous anatomy in bypass.

GSV Harvest for Arterial Bypass

The Gold Standard Bypass Conduit

Why GSV? Autologous vein has superior patency to prosthetic (PTFE) for below-knee reconstruction. Resistant to infection. Viable long-term.

Pre-op duplex mapping: Mandatory. Map GSV from ankle to groin. Note duplications, calibre (<3 mm = poor conduit), previous varicose vein surgery (harvested or stripped), superficial thrombophlebitis changes.

In-situ technique: GSV left in situ, valves destroyed with valvulotome, proximal anastomosis to CFA, distal to tibial artery. Tributaries ligated to prevent arteriovenous fistulae.

Reversed technique: GSV harvested, reversed (so valves allow antegrade flow), then interposed. Requires size match.

On the Table

Always harvest GSV from the IPSILATERAL leg if possible (shorter wound, reduces risk of bilateral leg morbidity). Check with consultant — some prefer contralateral GSV to preserve ipsilateral for future use.

Varicose Vein Surgery

Conventional Surgery — Sapheno-Femoral Ligation & Stripping

SFJ ligation (Trendelenburg operation):

Groin crease incision over SFJ (3.5 cm below and lateral to pubic tubercle). Flush ligation of GSV at CFV junction — divide ALL tributaries to prevent neovascularisation recurrence.

Stripping: GSV stripped from groin to knee (not ankle — sural nerve risk absent, saphenous nerve risk). Pin stripper or invagination stripper (reduces bruising).

Avulsions: Multiple stab avulsions of remaining varices through 2 mm incisions.

⚠ Exam Trap

Strip GSV to the KNEE only (not ankle) — the saphenous nerve becomes superficial below the knee and runs alongside the GSV → stripping to ankle causes saphenous nerve injury (medial calf/foot numbness) in up to 30%.

Endovenous Techniques

EVLA, RFA, Foam — Modern Alternatives to Open Surgery

Endovenous laser ablation (EVLA) / Radiofrequency ablation (RFA): Catheter introduced into GSV under ultrasound guidance at knee level, passed to SFJ under tumescent local anaesthesia, thermal energy closes vein. NICE recommended first-line for suitable veins (diameter 3–12 mm, straight segment ≥10 cm).

Ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy (UGFS): Polidocanol or sodium tetradecyl sulphate foam injected under USS. Used for smaller veins, recurrent varices, or SSV. Less durable than thermal ablation.

Cyanoacrylate glue / mechanochemical ablation (MOCA): Newer alternatives, no tumescent needed.

Complications of Varicose Vein Surgery

What to Know for Consent

• Saphenous nerve injury (medial calf numbness) — most common

• Sural nerve injury — with SSV surgery (lateral foot numbness)

• Lymphocoele/lymphatic injury — groin wound, beware at SFJ dissection

• DVT/PE — TEDS + early mobilisation. Significant risk in prolonged GA procedures

• Haematoma — along stripped vein track

• Recurrence — neovascularisation at groin (most common cause); missed tributaries; new disease

• Thermal injury to skin — with EVLA/RFA if inadequate tumescent

Anki Flash Cards

Where is the saphenofemoral junction (SFJ) and what is the key surgical landmark? flip ↓
3.5 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle. The GSV enters the CFV through the cribriform fascia (saphenous opening/fossa ovalis). The deep external pudendal artery crosses just below — a landmark for flush ligation.
Why is GSV stripping limited to the knee and not extended to the ankle? flip ↓
Below the knee, the saphenous nerve becomes superficial and runs alongside the GSV. Stripping to the ankle risks saphenous nerve injury (medial calf, medial foot numbness) in up to 30% of cases. Stripping to the knee avoids this — the GSV is subfascial in the thigh where nerve damage is less likely.
What is the CEAP C4b classification and what does it signify clinically? flip ↓
C4b = lipodermatosclerosis or atrophie blanche. Indicates severe chronic venous insufficiency with dermal fibrosis and subcutaneous fat sclerosis. Represents pre-ulceration stage — high risk of developing active ulcer (C6). Requires urgent vascular referral and compression therapy.
What is May-Thurner syndrome and what anatomical relationship causes it? flip ↓
May-Thurner (Cockett) syndrome = compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery at L5. Causes chronic left iliac vein stenosis/webs and predisposes to left-sided DVT, particularly in young women on oestrogen. Treated with catheter-directed thrombolysis + iliac vein stenting.
What are Cockett's perforators and why are they clinically important? flip ↓
Cockett I, II, III perforators are medial lower leg perforating veins posterior to the tibia. Their incompetence allows high-pressure reflux from the deep system into the superficial skin → lipodermatosclerosis and venous ulceration in the "gaiter area" (medial lower leg, above medial malleolus).
Which nerve is at risk during SSV surgery and what deficit does injury cause? flip ↓
The sural nerve runs alongside the SSV in the posterior calf. Injury causes sensory loss over the lateral foot and little toe. The sural nerve is purely sensory (no motor component) — no motor deficit, but the sensory loss can be troublesome and permanent.
What is the Giacomini vein? flip ↓
The Giacomini vein (thigh extension of SSV) is a communication between the SSV and the posteromedial thigh / GSV system. It allows SSV reflux to propagate into the thigh producing non-saphenous thigh varices. Important to identify on duplex to plan treatment — if Giacomini contributes to reflux, it must be addressed or recurrence occurs.
What is the "superficial femoral vein" and why is the name misleading? flip ↓
The "superficial femoral vein" is actually a DEEP vein — it is the femoral (deep) vein in the adductor canal, accompanying the superficial femoral artery. The name is misleading and dangerous: DVT here is a deep vein thrombosis requiring anticoagulation, not a superficial thrombophlebitis. Some centres now call it simply the "femoral vein" to avoid confusion.

Question 1 of 5A 45-year-old woman presents with varicose veins and a painful indurated discoloured area above the right medial malleolus. There is no ulceration. What is the CEAP classification?
A. C3
B. C4a
C. C5

Painful indurated skin over the medial gaiter area = lipodermatosclerosis. This is CEAP C4b. C4a = pigmentation/eczema. C5 = healed ulcer. C6 = active ulcer. C3 = oedema only. C4b is a fast-track referral indication under NICE guidelines as it represents pre-ulceration disease.
Question 2 of 5During a high saphenous ligation at the SFJ, the surgeon notices a large vein crossing the operative field approximately 1 cm inferior to the SFJ. This is most likely to be:
A. The profunda femoris vein
B. An accessory saphenous vein
C. The deep external pudendal artery
D. The superficial circumflex iliac vein
The deep external pudendal ARTERY crosses just inferior to the SFJ and is a key surgical landmark — it indicates the correct level for flush ligation. It must be identified and preserved (or ligated if necessary). Confusion here can lead to inadvertent injury. The superficial circumflex iliac and external pudendal veins are tributaries of the GSV at the SFJ, not crossing vessels.
Question 3 of 5A duplex scan for SSV incompetence shows the saphenopopliteal junction (SPJ) to be 4 cm above the popliteal skin crease. The surgeons fail to pre-operatively mark the SPJ. What is the most likely consequence during SSV surgery?
A. Injury to the common peroneal nerve
B. Inadvertent ligation of the popliteal artery
C. Incomplete ligation — SPJ not reached through standard popliteal crease incision — and recurrence
D. Injury to the tibial nerve
The SPJ level is highly variable. If a standard popliteal crease incision is used without pre-operative duplex marking, a high SPJ (4 cm above crease) may not be reached → incomplete ligation → SSV recurrence. This is one of the most common causes of SSV surgery failure. Pre-operative duplex marking is mandatory in UK vascular surgical practice.
Question 4 of 5A 35-year-old woman taking the combined oral contraceptive pill presents with acute left leg swelling and pain. CT venography confirms extensive left common iliac vein DVT. What anatomical mechanism predisposed to this?
A. Left iliac vein is naturally narrower than the right
B. The right common iliac artery compresses the left common iliac vein at L5 (May-Thurner syndrome)
C. The left CIV has fewer valves than the right
D. The left CIV is longer, increasing transit time and stasis
May-Thurner (Cockett) syndrome: the right common iliac artery crosses anteriorly over the left common iliac vein, causing chronic compression at L5. This creates intraluminal webs/fibrosis and venous hypertension, predisposing to left iliac DVT — particularly triggered by oestrogen-related hypercoagulability. Treatment: CDT + iliac vein stenting.
Question 5 of 5A pre-operative duplex scan for femoropopliteal bypass identifies a duplicated GSV: one segment is 5 mm diameter and one is 2.5 mm. The patient's ipsilateral GSV was previously harvested to the knee for a CABG. What is the most appropriate bypass conduit strategy?
A. Use the 2.5 mm GSV segment — it will dilate under arterial pressure
B. Use arm vein (basilic or cephalic) or PTFE with distal vein cuff for below-knee anastomosis
C. Use contralateral GSV which was not harvested
D. Use PTFE only — equivalent results to vein below knee
The 2.5 mm GSV is too small (<3 mm threshold for reliable conduit). The contralateral GSV should be preserved for future procedures if possible (e.g., future CABG, contralateral bypass). Arm vein (cephalic/basilic) is the preferred alternative autologous conduit. If prosthetic MUST be used below knee, a distal vein cuff (Miller/Taylor) improves patency. PTFE alone below the knee has significantly inferior patency to vein.
