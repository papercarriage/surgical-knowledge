Why HPB anatomy matters

The hepatobiliary region is one of the most anatomically treacherous in surgery. Failure to understand it causes bile duct injuries — one of the most devastating complications in general surgery. Master this before your first HPB rotation.

The three spaces you must own

Calot's Triangle Critical

The operative triangle bounded by the cystic duct (inferiorly), the common hepatic duct (medially), and the inferior surface of the liver (superiorly). The cystic artery runs within or near this triangle in most patients — but critically, the anatomy here varies in up to 24% of individuals.

Critical view of safety (CVS): The modern standard before dividing any structure in laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Two criteria must be met: (1) the hepatocystic triangle is cleared of fat and fibrous tissue, and (2) the lower third of the gallbladder is separated from the liver bed, so only two structures are seen entering the gallbladder.

Hepatoduodenal Ligament Critical

The free edge of the lesser omentum — the key surgical highway of the abdomen. Contains the portal triad: portal vein (posterior), hepatic artery (left anterior), and common bile duct (right anterior). Remember VAN from posterior to anterior: Vein, Artery, Nerve/duct — though the duct's relationship with the artery varies.

The Pringle manoeuvre compresses this ligament between finger and thumb to control hepatic inflow during liver surgery.

Lesser Sac (Omental Bursa) Important

The potential space posterior to the stomach and lesser omentum. Entered through the Foramen of Winslow (bounded by: hepatoduodenal ligament anteriorly, IVC posteriorly, liver superiorly, duodenum inferiorly). Surgically accessed for posterior gastric, splenic, and pancreatic body/tail operations. Pseudocysts from pancreatitis collect here.

Couinaud's Segmental Anatomy

The liver is divided into 8 functional segments based on portal pedicles and hepatic veins. Each segment has its own vascular inflow (portal vein + hepatic artery branch) and outflow (hepatic vein). This is the basis for anatomical hepatic resection — you can remove any segment or combination while preserving the rest.

The 3 hepatic veins — your surgical planes

Right hepatic vein: divides right lobe into anterior (V, VIII) and posterior (VI, VII) sectors.
Middle hepatic vein: the true anatomical midplane dividing left and right lobes. Runs in the principal plane (Cantlie's line) from gallbladder fossa to IVC.
Left hepatic vein: divides left lobe into medial (IV) and lateral (II, III) sectors.

Segment I (caudate lobe) is unique — it drains directly into the IVC via multiple small veins and has dual portal supply. It's preserved or sacrificed based on these independent connections.

Port placement — laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Standard 4-port technique

10mm umbilical port: Camera port. Insert Hassan or Veress depending on prior surgery history. Risk of preperitoneal placement if technique is poor.

5mm epigastric port: Liver retractor. Place to the right of falciform to avoid inferior epigastric vessels and minimise liver injury.

5mm right flank port: Working port for dissection. Place in midclavicular line level with umbilicus.

5mm anterior axillary port: Gallbladder retraction. Most lateral port — keep it lateral enough to allow fundus-first retraction.

Operative tip

In laparoscopic cholecystectomy, retract the gallbladder fundus superiorly and laterally — not just cephalad. This opens Calot's triangle and aligns the cystic duct perpendicular to the CBD, reducing the risk of misidentification.

Anatomical variants

Variants are not rare — they are expected. The surgeon who is surprised by variant anatomy has not prepared sufficiently. Know the frequency and the consequence of each.

Biliary variants

Critical point

A replaced or accessory right hepatic artery from the SMA runs posterior to the portal vein and behind the CBD in the hepatoduodenal ligament. You will feel it pulsating during pancreaticoduodenectomy. Always check for it — ligation = right lobe ischaemia.

Danger zones

These are the moments in HPB surgery where good anatomy knowledge prevents disasters. Study these scenarios actively, not passively.

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Danger zone 1

Tenting the CBD: If the gallbladder is retracted too cephalad (not laterally), it tents the CBD upward into the operative field. The CBD then appears horizontal and can be mistaken for the cystic duct. This is the single most common mechanism of major bile duct injury.

Danger zone 2

The short cystic duct: If the cystic duct is <5mm long, clips placed on it may partially occlude the CBD. Always place clips with the full duct visible on both sides of each clip.

Danger zone 3

Bleeding from the cystic artery: Attempting to control haemorrhage with blind clipping in Calot's triangle is how hepatic arteries are ligated. Convert to open, compress with a sponge, and get exposure before any vascular control.

Hepatic resection

Danger zone 4

Middle hepatic vein in right hepatectomy: During parenchymal transection along Cantlie's line, the middle hepatic vein runs in the plane. Injury causes massive haemorrhage and potential air embolism. The left lobe's venous drainage depends on it — sacrificing it may devascularise segment IV.

Danger zone 5

Short right hepatic vein: The right hepatic vein has a very short extrahepatic course (0–15mm) before entering the IVC. Stapler misfiring during division is possible. Identify it with Pringle, verify its length with intraoperative ultrasound before dividing.

Pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple)

Danger zone 6

Portal vein tunnel: The neck of the pancreas is tunnelled off the portal vein and SMV. Tributaries (especially the gastrocolic trunk / vein of Henle) entering from the right are short and tear easily. Stay on the vein surface and ligate each tributary individually — never avulse.

Danger zone 7

SMA first approach: In the artery-first technique, the SMA is identified at the base of the mesentery before dividing the pancreatic neck. This allows assessment of resectability (posterior pancreatic margin) and early arterial control. Failure to identify the SMA can lead to inadvertent injury and catastrophic haemorrhage.

Intraoperative principle

When in doubt — stop, reassess, get help. A converted laparoscopic cholecystectomy is never a failure. A bile duct injury is. The decision to convert is a surgical decision, not a defeat.
