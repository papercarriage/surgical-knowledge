Larynx & Airway Anatomy

The larynx is a cartilaginous valve, a vocal organ and the narrowest part of the adult airway. Know its framework, its nerve supply and its landmarks, because every emergency airway and every thyroid operation depends on them.

Framework

Cartilages

• Unpaired: thyroid, cricoid, epiglottis. Paired: arytenoid, corniculate, cuneiform

• Cricoid is the only complete cartilaginous ring. Its lower border (C6) marks the start of the trachea and oesophagus

• Vertebral levels: hyoid C3, thyroid cartilage C4–C5, cricoid C6

• Cricothyroid membrane joins thyroid and cricoid cartilages: roughly 1 cm tall and 3 cm wide, subcutaneous and palpable

Regions

• Supraglottis: epiglottis to the ventricles (includes false cords)

• Glottis: true vocal cords and anterior commissure. Narrowest part of the adult airway

• Subglottis: from about 1 cm below the cords to the lower border of cricoid

Sensory Supply and Blood

• Above the cords: internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve (pierces thyrohyoid membrane). Loss gives aspiration risk

• Below the cords: recurrent laryngeal nerve

• Arteries: superior laryngeal (from superior thyroid) and inferior laryngeal (from inferior thyroid)

• Lymph: supraglottic drains to levels II–III; subglottic to level VI and lower deep cervical; the glottis has sparse lymphatics, so early glottic cancer rarely has nodes

⚠️ Exam Trap

The posterior cricoarytenoid is the only abductor. All intrinsic muscles are supplied by the RLN except cricothyroid (EBSLN). Sensation above the cords is from the superior laryngeal nerve, below from the RLN.

Key Facts & Numbers

Trachea numbers, paediatric differences and cord positions.

Trachea

• Length about 10–11 cm, 16–20 C-shaped rings; C6 to T4/5 (carina at the sternal angle level)

• Isthmus of the thyroid lies over rings 2–4

• Right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical, so aspirated material and misplaced tubes go right

• Posterior wall is membranous (trachealis) with oesophagus behind

Paediatric Airway

Differences

• Larynx sits higher (C3–C4); epiglottis is large and omega-shaped

• Narrowest point is the cricoid (funnel shaped), not the glottis, so uncuffed tubes were historically used

• Small oedema causes large resistance rise (radius to the fourth power)

⚠️ Exam Trap

The old teaching (Semon's law) that abductors are more vulnerable than adductors is not reliable clinically. Do not use it to predict cord position; examine the cord.

Surgical Relevance

Surgical and emergency airway access, and what lies in the way.

Surgical Airway

Cricothyroidotomy (can't intubate, can't oxygenate)

• Identify the cricothyroid membrane by palpation (laryngeal handshake). If not palpable, make a vertical midline skin incision and dissect to find it

• Scalpel, then bougie, then 6.0 cuffed tube (Difficult Airway Society scalpel-bougie-tube technique)

• Contraindicated in young children (needle cricothyroidotomy or front-of-neck access by experienced operator)

Tracheostomy

• Incision midway between cricoid and sternal notch. Divide or retract the thyroid isthmus. Window in rings 2–3 (avoid ring 1: cricoid injury, subglottic stenosis)

• Dangers: anterior jugular veins, thyroid isthmus, thyroidea ima, left brachiocephalic vein (high in children), innominate artery

• Sentinel bleed days later suggests tracheo-innominate fistula: over-inflate cuff, finger in the wound to compress anteriorly, to theatre

Other Operative Points

Know These

• RLN enters the larynx behind the cricothyroid joint, deep to the inferior constrictor. The ligament of Berry is the high-risk zone in thyroidectomy

• Prolonged intubation (cuff pressure, beyond 7–10 days) causes subglottic stenosis; this is why early tracheostomy is considered

• Post-extubation stridor: oedema, cord palsy, laryngospasm, haematoma. Always check neck after thyroid surgery

• Bilateral RLN palsy after thyroidectomy: reintubate or surgical airway, then cord-lateralisation options later

⚠️ Exam Trap

In tracheostomy, a pulsatile bleed from the tracheostomy site days later is a tracheo-innominate fistula until proven otherwise, not granulation tissue. Do not simply pack and observe.

Which is the only complete cartilaginous ring of the airway? flip ↓
The cricoid. Its lower border (C6) marks the start of the trachea and oesophagus.
Which is the only abductor of the vocal cords? flip ↓
The posterior cricoarytenoid (RLN). All other intrinsic muscles adduct, tense or relax.
Which intrinsic laryngeal muscle is not supplied by the RLN? flip ↓
Cricothyroid, supplied by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. It tenses the cords and raises pitch.
What supplies sensation above vs below the vocal cords? flip ↓
Above: internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. Below: recurrent laryngeal nerve.
What are the three regions of the larynx? flip ↓
Supraglottis, glottis (cords), subglottis (about 1 cm below the cords to the lower border of cricoid).
Narrowest part of the adult vs paediatric airway? flip ↓
Adult: glottis (between the cords). Child: cricoid (subglottis).
Why does an aspirated foreign body usually enter the right bronchus? flip ↓
The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left.
At which tracheal rings is tracheostomy performed and why avoid ring 1? flip ↓
Rings 2–3. Ring 1 is next to the cricoid; injury risks subglottic stenosis.
Landmark and technique for emergency front-of-neck access? flip ↓
Cricothyroid membrane between thyroid and cricoid cartilages. Scalpel-bougie-tube; vertical skin incision if landmarks are not palpable.
Cord position after unilateral RLN palsy? flip ↓
Paramedian. Hoarse, breathy voice and weak cough; airway is usually adequate. Bilateral palsy gives stridor.
What does a sentinel bleed after tracheostomy suggest? flip ↓
Tracheo-innominate fistula. Hyperinflate cuff, digital compression anteriorly via the stoma, urgent theatre.
Vertebral levels of hyoid, thyroid cartilage and cricoid? flip ↓
C3, C4–C5, C6 respectively.

Question 1 of 5A patient develops stridor immediately after total thyroidectomy. Laryngoscopy shows both cords fixed in the paramedian position. Which nerve injury explains this?
A. A. Bilateral external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve
B. B. Bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve
C. C. Bilateral internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve
D. D. Right vagus nerve only
Bilateral RLN injury paralyses the posterior cricoarytenoid abductors on both sides, leaving the cords paramedian and the glottis narrow. This is an airway emergency. EBSLN injury affects pitch only; internal SLN injury affects sensation.
Question 2 of 5Which muscle is the sole abductor of the vocal cords?
A. A. Cricothyroid
B. B. Lateral cricoarytenoid
C. C. Posterior cricoarytenoid
D. D. Thyroarytenoid
The posterior cricoarytenoid rotates the arytenoids outwards to open the glottis. Cricothyroid tenses; lateral cricoarytenoid and transverse arytenoid adduct; thyroarytenoid relaxes the cords.
Question 3 of 5In a can't intubate, can't oxygenate scenario in an adult, where is front-of-neck access made?
A. A. Through the thyroid isthmus
B. B. Between the hyoid and thyroid cartilage
C. C. Through tracheal ring 1
D. D. Through the cricothyroid membrane
The cricothyroid membrane is subcutaneous and avascular relative to the other options, lying between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages. The isthmus is vascular and lies over rings 2–4.
Question 4 of 5Why is the narrowest part of a young child's airway clinically important?
A. A. It is at the glottis, so bag-mask ventilation is difficult
B. B. It is at the cricoid, so small mucosal oedema produces marked obstruction
C. C. It is at the carina, so tubes enter the left bronchus
D. D. It is at the epiglottis, so intubation is always difficult
In children the cricoid ring is the narrowest point. Airflow resistance rises with the fourth power of radius reduction, so a little oedema from croup, trauma or an oversized tube causes severe obstruction and later subglottic stenosis.
Question 5 of 5Five days after tracheostomy a patient has a small bright-red bleed from the stoma that stops spontaneously. What is the most appropriate response?
A. A. Treat as a tracheo-innominate fistula and urgently involve the surgical and airway teams
B. B. Reassure; it is granulation tissue
C. C. Change the tube and discharge
D. D. Start oral antibiotics
A sentinel bleed is the warning of a tracheo-innominate fistula (erosion of the innominate artery by the tube tip or cuff). Mortality is high if missed. Hyperinflate the cuff, apply digital pressure anteriorly, and get the patient to theatre.
