Lower Limb Arterial Anatomy

The lower limb arterial tree is the battleground of peripheral arterial disease, acute limb ischaemia, and vascular reconstruction. Every level of disease determines the operative strategy — know the anatomy, know the operation.

The Femoral Triangle

NAVL — Lateral to Medial at the Femoral Triangle

Nerve (femoral) — Artery (CFA) — Vein (CFV) — Lymphatics

Boundaries: Inguinal ligament (superior), sartorius (lateral), adductor longus (medial), iliopsoas + pectineus (floor).

Clinical Pearl

The femoral vein lies medial to the artery — risk of inadvertent venous puncture when accessing CFA for angiography. Always compress above and below after sheath removal.

Hunter's (Adductor) Canal

Adductor Canal — SFA's Vulnerable Zone

Triangular fibromuscular tunnel in middle third of thigh. Contains SFA, femoral vein, saphenous nerve, nerve to vastus medialis.

Roof: sartorius. Lateral wall: vastus medialis. Medial wall: adductor longus / adductor magnus.

SFA exits at the adductor hiatus → becomes popliteal artery. This is a common disease transition point — SFA disease often terminates here.

Popliteal Fossa

Popliteal Fossa Contents — NAVEL (Superficial to Deep)

Nerve (tibial, then common peroneal) — Artery — Vein — Empty space — Lymphatics

The popliteal artery is the deepest structure in the fossa, directly on the posterior capsule of the knee joint. This makes it vulnerable in posterior knee dislocations.

⚠ Exam Trap

Popliteal artery is the most POSTERIOR structure in the popliteal fossa (deepest = closest to bone). Common mistake: thinking the nerve is deepest. Nerve is most superficial.

The Trifurcation

Popliteal Trifurcation — Below the Knee Joint

At the lower border of popliteus muscle, the popliteal artery divides into:

1. Anterior tibial — passes anteriorly through interosseous membrane immediately

2. Tibioperoneal trunk (2–3 cm) → then splits into:

   • Posterior tibial (medial, continues distally)

   • Peroneal (lateral, along fibula)

Collateral Circulation — Why It Matters

Key Collateral Pathways

SFA occlusion: Profunda femoris → perforators → geniculate anastomosis → popliteal (this is why chronic SFA occlusion can be asymptomatic)

Iliac occlusion: Internal iliac (gluteal) ↔ superficial femoral collaterals; lumbar → circumflex iliac

Aortic occlusion (Leriche): IMA → superior rectal → internal iliac (Winslow's pathway); intercostals → lumbar → iliac

Clinical Pearl

A well-collateralised SFA occlusion produces claudication at ~200m. The same occlusion with poor profunda = rest pain or tissue loss. Profunda quality determines ischaemia severity.

Key Facts & Numbers

High-yield facts for MCQs, clinical discussions, and operative consent.

Landmark Numbers

Must-Know Measurements

• Aortic bifurcation: L4 (level of umbilicus, iliac crests)

• CFA begins at: midpoint of inguinal ligament (ASIS to pubic tubercle)

• PFA origin: 3–4 cm below inguinal ligament, posterolateral to CFA

• Adductor hiatus: junction of middle and lower thirds of thigh

• Popliteal trifurcation: lower border of popliteus muscle

• Dorsalis pedis pulse: lateral to extensor hallucis longus tendon

• Posterior tibial pulse: behind medial malleolus

Eponyms & Classifications

Named Structures

Hunter's canal = adductor canal (John Hunter, 18th C surgeon-anatomist)

Scarpa's triangle = femoral triangle

Leriche syndrome = aorto-iliac occlusion: buttock claudication + erectile dysfunction + absent femoral pulses

Winslow's pathway = collateral route via IMA in aortic occlusion

TASC II classification = morphological grading of aorto-iliac and femoro-popliteal lesions (A–D) guiding endo vs open

⚠ Exam Trap

Dorsalis pedis is congenitally absent in 5–10% of the population. Never diagnose occlusion on absent DP alone — always check PT. Posterior tibial is the more reliable pulse.

Anatomical Variants

Clinically Relevant Variants

• High bifurcation of CFA (~10%): PFA originates above inguinal ligament — complicates endarterectomy

• Absent/hypoplastic peroneal: limits bypass options in CLI

• Persistent sciatic artery: rare; CFA hypoplastic, limb supplied by internal iliac → sciatic nerve → popliteal

• Popliteal artery entrapment: aberrant medial head of gastrocnemius — young athletes, exertional claudication

Surgical Relevance

How this anatomy maps directly onto operations you will assist with on the vascular list.

Femoral Endarterectomy / Access

Common Femoral Artery — The Workhorse Access Point

Incision: Longitudinal or oblique over femoral pulse, centred on mid-inguinal point. Beware lymphatics — ligate to prevent lymphocoele.

Layers: Skin → Camper's fascia → Scarpa's fascia → cribriform fascia → CFA

Endarterectomy end-point: Tacking sutures at distal endpoint to prevent intimal flap. Patch closure (vein or Dacron) preferred to prevent restenosis.

Profundaplasty: Extending endarterectomy into PFA origin — critical when SFA is occluded and profunda is the only outflow. Improves inflow to collaterals.

On the Table

Feel for the profunda pulse after reperfusion — a strong PFA pulse is a good prognostic sign even if SFA remains occluded.

Femoropopliteal Bypass

SFA Disease — Bypass Strategy

Inflow: CFA or PFA (profunda-popliteal bypass in selected cases)

Outflow targets (proximal to distal preference):

  1. Above-knee popliteal (P1 segment) — best patency, synthetic acceptable

  2. Below-knee popliteal (P3 segment) — vein graft preferred

  3. Tibial/peroneal — crural bypass, vein graft mandatory

Conduit choice: Ipsilateral GSV (long saphenous) = gold standard. Reversed or in-situ (valvulotome). If unavailable: arm vein, PTFE + vein cuff (Miller cuff / Taylor patch) at distal anastomosis.

⚠ Exam Trap

PTFE to below-knee popliteal has significantly worse patency than vein. Always use autologous vein below the knee if at all possible.

Embolectomy

Fogarty Catheter Technique via Femoral Access

Indication: Acute limb ischaemia (embolic > thrombotic) — 6Ps within 6 hours ideally

Access: CFA via longitudinal arteriotomy. Transverse arteriotomy if no concurrent endarterectomy planned.

Technique: Size 4F proximally (iliac), size 3F distally (tibials). Inflate gently — over-inflation strips intima. Pass until no further clot retrieved AND distal back-bleed achieved.

Completion: On-table angiogram to confirm runoff. Failed embolectomy → consider thrombolysis or distal bypass.

Clinical Pearl

BKA: posterior flap = 2/3 of limb circumference long. The gastrocnemius provides the bulk of the posterior flap — its blood supply (sural arteries from popliteal) must be intact.

Popliteal Aneurysm

Popliteal Artery Aneurysm — Surgical Approach

Most common peripheral aneurysm (70% of all). Bilateral in 50%. Associated with AAA in 40%.

Medial approach: Patient supine, knee slightly flexed. Incision medial to sartorius. Identifies P2/P3 popliteal. Preferred for elective bypass.

Posterior approach: Patient prone. S-shaped incision in popliteal fossa. Used for large aneurysms requiring sac opening.

Repair: Proximal/distal ligation + vein bypass (exclude + bypass, not resection).

Anki Flash Cards

Click each card to reveal the answer.

At what vertebral level does the aorta bifurcate? flip ↓
L4 — at the level of the umbilicus and iliac crests. The right common iliac crosses the right ureter here.
What is the NAVL mnemonic and what does it describe? flip ↓
Nerve–Artery–Vein–Lymphatics, lateral to medial order in the femoral triangle. The femoral nerve is most lateral, lymphatics most medial.
Where does the profunda femoris originate and why does it matter in PAD? flip ↓
3–4 cm below the inguinal ligament, posterolateral to CFA. In SFA occlusion, the profunda is the primary collateral supply to the lower limb via perforators to the geniculate anastomosis. A patent, wide profunda can compensate for total SFA occlusion.
Which structure is deepest (most posterior) in the popliteal fossa? flip ↓
The popliteal ARTERY — lying directly on the posterior knee capsule. Order from superficial to deep: tibial nerve → popliteal vein → popliteal artery (NAVEL mnemonic).
What is Leriche syndrome? Name the triad. flip ↓
Aorto-iliac occlusion. Triad: (1) Bilateral buttock/thigh claudication, (2) Erectile dysfunction, (3) Absent/reduced femoral pulses. Treatment: aorto-bifemoral bypass or aorto-iliac stenting.
Where is the posterior tibial pulse felt and what does its absence signify? flip ↓
Behind the medial malleolus. Absence suggests posterior tibial artery or trifurcation disease. More reliable than dorsalis pedis (congenitally absent in 5–10%).
What conduit is preferred for a below-knee popliteal bypass and why? flip ↓
Ipsilateral great saphenous vein (GSV). Autologous vein has superior patency rates below the knee compared to prosthetic (PTFE). Vein can be reversed or used in-situ with a valvulotome.
What is popliteal artery entrapment syndrome? flip ↓
Compression of the popliteal artery by an aberrant medial head of gastrocnemius (or popliteus). Affects young athletes. Presents with exertional calf claudication. Diagnosed by dynamic MRA or duplex (ankle plantarflexion provocative test). Treated surgically by releasing the aberrant muscle head.
What blood supply does a below-knee amputation posterior flap rely on? flip ↓
Sural arteries (branches of popliteal artery) supplying the gastrocnemius muscle. The long posterior myocutaneous flap (Burgess technique) depends on an intact popliteal → sural artery axis.
What is the last vessel to occlude in severe multi-level PAD and why? flip ↓
The peroneal artery — it runs in a protected fibro-osseous tunnel along the fibula and has less atherosclerotic burden than tibial vessels. Often the only patent crural vessel in critical limb ischaemia and can be used as a distal bypass target.

Question 1 of 5A 68-year-old man presents with left buttock and thigh claudication at 100 metres, absent left femoral pulse, and impotence. His right femoral pulse is also absent. Which eponymous syndrome describes this presentation?
A. Hunter's syndrome
B. Leriche syndrome
C. Fontaine stage IIb
D. Raynaud's phenomenon
Leriche syndrome = aorto-iliac occlusion, characterised by the classic triad of bilateral buttock/thigh claudication, erectile dysfunction, and absent femoral pulses. Hunter's canal relates to the SFA, not the aorto-iliac segment. Fontaine IIb describes calf claudication at <200m, which is femoro-popliteal disease.
Question 2 of 5During a femoral embolectomy, you pass a Fogarty catheter proximally and retrieve clot. You now pass distally and no further clot returns. Which vessel should you use a size 3F catheter to selectively cannulate for distal clot retrieval?
A. Profunda femoris
B. Popliteal artery
C. Common femoral artery
D. Tibial vessels individually
After initial distal embolectomy, each tibial vessel (anterior tibial, posterior tibial, peroneal) must be cannulated individually with a smaller 3F catheter. The three crural vessels take separate angles and one pass of a 4F catheter cannot reliably clear all three. On-table angiography confirms adequate runoff.
Question 3 of 5A 45-year-old marathon runner presents with calf pain reproducible on running but absent at rest. Pedal pulses are normal at rest. Duplex shows compression of the popliteal artery on plantarflexion. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Chronic exertional compartment syndrome
B. DVT
C. Popliteal artery entrapment syndrome
D. Atherosclerotic PAD
Popliteal artery entrapment is caused by an aberrant medial head of gastrocnemius compressing the popliteal artery. It presents in young, fit patients with exertional claudication, normal resting pulses, and positive provocation tests. Duplex or MRA confirms dynamic compression. Treatment is surgical release of the aberrant muscle.
Question 4 of 5You are planning a below-knee amputation (BKA) for a diabetic patient with ischaemic heel ulceration. The posterior tibial artery is occluded. Which vessel's patency is most critical for flap viability using the Burgess technique?
A. Anterior tibial artery
B. Popliteal artery (via sural branches)
C. Dorsalis pedis artery
D. Profunda femoris artery
The Burgess BKA uses a long posterior myocutaneous flap based on the gastrocnemius muscle. The gastrocnemius is supplied by the sural arteries, which are branches of the popliteal artery. A patent popliteal → sural axis is essential for flap survival. The tibial and dorsalis pedis arteries are distal to the level of amputation and irrelevant to flap perfusion.
Question 5 of 5A patient with bilateral popliteal aneurysms is found to have a 5.5 cm AAA incidentally. What is the most accurate statement about this association?
A. Popliteal aneurysms are associated with AAA in 10% of cases
B. Femoral aneurysms are more common than popliteal aneurysms
C. Popliteal aneurysms are associated with AAA in ~40% of cases and are bilateral in ~50%
D. Popliteal aneurysms rarely cause limb loss
Popliteal aneurysms are the most common peripheral arterial aneurysm. Key associations: bilateral in ~50%, associated with AAA in ~40%. All patients with a popliteal aneurysm should be screened for AAA. They commonly present with acute limb ischaemia from thromboembolism (not rupture) — limb loss risk is significant without treatment.
