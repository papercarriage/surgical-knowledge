Aorta & Iliac Anatomy

The aorta is the highway of systemic circulation. Its aneurysmal degeneration, occlusive disease, and the complex anatomy relevant to open and endovascular repair make this essential knowledge for any vascular posting.

Visceral Branch Levels

Vertebral Levels — Must Know

• Coeliac axis: T12/L1 — foregut supply (stomach, duodenum, liver, spleen, pancreas)

• Superior mesenteric artery (SMA): L1 — midgut (distal duodenum → splenic flexure)

• Renal arteries: L1/L2 (right renal arises slightly higher, longer — crosses IVC posteriorly)

• Inferior mesenteric artery (IMA): L3 — hindgut (splenic flexure → rectum)

• Aortic bifurcation: L4 (umbilicus level)

Mnemonic

"Coeliac 12, SMA 1, renal 1, IMA 3, bifurcate 4" — T12, L1, L1, L3, L4

Relations of the Abdominal Aorta

Anterior Relations — What You Divide to Get There

• Lesser omentum and stomach (upper)

• Pancreas (body crosses anterior to aorta at L1/L2) — the left renal vein crosses between SMA and aorta

• Third part of duodenum (crosses aorta anteriorly at L3) — critical landmark for open AAA

• Small bowel mesentery root

• Left renal vein — crosses ANTERIOR to aorta to drain into IVC. Must be mobilised (or rarely divided) for proximal control in suprarenal AAA.

⚠ Exam Trap

The left renal vein crosses ANTERIOR to the aorta (not the right). It can be divided during suprarenal aortic surgery if collateral drainage is adequate (gonadal + adrenal tributaries). The RIGHT renal vein is short and drains directly into IVC.

The Iliac System

Common, Internal & External Iliac Arteries

Common iliac (CIA): ~5 cm long. Ureter crosses at the bifurcation — "water under the bridge." Bifurcates at pelvic brim into EIA and IIA.

Internal iliac (IIA / hypogastric): Posterior division → gluteal, pudendal, obturator. Anterior division → visceral branches (bladder, uterus, rectum). Must preserve at least one IIA during AAA/aorto-iliac repair to prevent buttock claudication and impotence.

External iliac (EIA): Becomes CFA at the inguinal ligament. Gives inferior epigastric artery (landmark for laparoscopic anatomy — medial to deep ring).

EVAR Anatomical Zones

Infrarenal AAA — EVAR Eligibility Anatomy

Proximal neck (must have ≥15 mm): Segment of normal aorta below lowest renal artery. Must be <28–32 mm diameter, <60° angulation, no thrombus/calcification >50% circumference.

Iliac landing zones: CIA must be ≥10 mm long, <20 mm diameter. If CIA aneurysmal → iliac limb extended to EIA (with IIA embolisation — risk of buttock ischaemia).

Access vessels: CFA/EIA must accommodate device sheath (14–20F). Calcified/tortuous iliacs may prevent EVAR → open repair or hybrid.

Clinical Pearl

~60% of AAAs are anatomically suitable for standard infrarenal EVAR. Complex endovascular techniques (fenestrated/branched EVAR) extend this to juxtarenal and suprarenal aneurysms.

Crawford Classification (TAAA)

Thoracoabdominal Aortic Aneurysms — Crawford I–V

Type I: Descending thoracic + upper abdominal aorta (to renals)

Type II: Descending thoracic + entire abdominal aorta (most extensive — highest risk)

Type III: Lower descending thoracic + abdominal aorta

Type IV: Infradiaphragmatic abdominal aorta only

Type V: Lower descending thoracic + suprarenal abdominal (some classifications)

Spinal Cord Ischaemia Risk

Highest in Type I and II (involves T8–12 intercostals supplying artery of Adamkiewicz). Preventive strategies: CSF drainage, staged repair, motor-evoked potential monitoring.

IMA & Collateral Circulation

IMA — Preserve or Ligate?

The IMA (at L3) supplies the hindgut. During open AAA repair it is commonly ligated at its origin. This is safe if:

• IMA back-pressure >40 mmHg (patent Meandering mesenteric artery / arc of Riolan)

• Good SMA–IMA collateral via the marginal artery of Drummond

Reimplantation of IMA indicated if: poor back pressure, Meandering mesenteric artery seen on imaging, sigmoid ischaemia risk (especially if IIA compromised).

⚠ Exam Trap

The arc of Riolan and marginal artery of Drummond are different collateral pathways. The arc of Riolan (Meandering mesenteric) is a direct SMA–IMA anastomosis in the mesentery — a large, tortuous vessel seen on CT = sign of SMA or IMA stenosis.

Surgical Relevance

Open AAA repair and EVAR anatomy — what's relevant when you're scrubbed.

Open AAA Repair — Transperitoneal Approach

Midline Laparotomy — Steps to Aortic Control

Incision: Midline xiphoid to pubis (or lower midline for infrarenal)

Bowel mobilisation: Small bowel eviscerated to the right. Transverse colon reflected superiorly.

Posterior peritoneum: Incised over the aorta. The left renal vein crossing anteriorly is the landmark for the infrarenal neck.

Proximal control: Below renal veins (infrarenal) — finger dissection clears tissue around neck for clamp placement.

Distal control: CIA clamped bilaterally. If aneurysm extends to bifurcation → EIA and IIA controlled separately.

Graft: Tube graft (if bifurcation normal) or bifurcated graft to CFA/CIA. Dacron or PTFE. Graft covered with aneurysm sac to prevent aorto-enteric fistula.

Retroperitoneal Approach

Left Flank Approach — Advantages & When Used

Left lateral position, flank incision through 10th/11th rib bed. Peritoneum swept anteriorly to expose retroperitoneum.

Advantages: Better exposure of proximal (suprarenal/juxtarenal) aorta. Avoids bowel handling. Better for hostile abdomen (previous laparotomy), horseshoe kidney, inflammatory AAA.

Disadvantage: Difficult right iliac access. Cannot inspect intraabdominal organs.

EVAR — Procedure Overview

Endovascular AAA Repair — Key Steps

Access: Bilateral CFA via open cutdown or percutaneous (ProGlide). Fluoroscopy guidance throughout.

Main body deployment: Ipsilateral (usually right) — deployed just below lowest renal artery under fluoroscopy with marker pigtail at renals.

Contralateral limb: Cannulated from left CFA, gate identified, iliac limb deployed.

Completion angiography: Confirms exclusion of aneurysm sac, no endoleak, renal arteries patent, no limb kinks.

Endoleak Classification

Type I: Seal zone failure (proximal Ia, distal Ib) — must treat

Type II: Back-fill from branch vessel (IMA, lumbar) — observe if sac not growing

Type III: Graft fabric tear / junction separation — must treat

Type IV: Graft porosity (older grafts) — usually self-limiting

Type V (endotension): Sac expansion without visible endoleak

Ruptured AAA

rAAA — Anatomy of Tamponade and Operative Strategy

Posterior rupture: Contained by retroperitoneum — temporary tamponade, time for imaging. Classic triad: hypotension + back/flank pain + pulsatile mass.

Anterior rupture: Free intraperitoneal → rapid exsanguination, collapse.

Immediate proximal control: Aortic occlusion balloon (REBOA — Zone III: aorta at L3) inserted endovascularly, or manual aortic compression at diaphragmatic hiatus on opening abdomen (T12 level, two finger widths below xiphoid).

EVAR for rAAA: IMPROVE trial — EVAR and open comparable outcomes for rAAA; EVAR preferred where anatomy suitable and haemodynamically stable enough for transfer.

At what vertebral level does the aorta give off the coeliac axis, SMA, and renal arteries? flip ↓
Coeliac: T12/L1. SMA: L1. Renal arteries: L1/L2. IMA: L3. Bifurcation: L4. Mnemonic: "Coeliac 12, SMA 1, renals 1, IMA 3, bifurcate 4."
What is the elective repair threshold for infrarenal AAA in men? In women? flip ↓
Men: 5.5 cm. Women: 5.0–5.2 cm (lower because the female aorta is smaller at baseline — a 5 cm AAA represents more relative dilatation). Both per NICE and SVS guidelines.
The left renal vein crosses anterior to what structure and why is this relevant to open aortic surgery? flip ↓
The left renal vein crosses ANTERIOR to the aorta to drain into the IVC. It is the landmark for the infrarenal aortic neck during open AAA repair. It must be carefully mobilised (or occasionally divided if collaterals adequate) to achieve proximal control for juxtarenal AAA.
What is the NHS AAA screening programme? Who is screened and at what threshold is referral made? flip ↓
Single abdominal ultrasound for all men aged 65. Results: <3 cm discharge; 3–4.4 cm annual surveillance; 4.5–5.4 cm 3-monthly surveillance; ≥5.5 cm refer to vascular surgery for repair consideration.
What is a Type II endoleak and how is it managed? flip ↓
Back-fill into the aneurysm sac from branch vessels (most commonly IMA or lumbar arteries). Management: observe if sac is stable or shrinking. Intervene (coil embolisation via IMA/lumbar, or direct sac puncture) only if the sac continues to enlarge (>5 mm growth).
What is Leriche syndrome? Where is the occlusion? flip ↓
Leriche syndrome = aorto-iliac occlusion (at or just above the bifurcation). Triad: bilateral buttock/thigh claudication, erectile dysfunction, absent femoral pulses. Named after René Leriche. Treatment: aorto-bifemoral bypass or bilateral iliac stenting.
What is the Crawford Type II TAAA and why does it carry the highest risk? flip ↓
Crawford Type II extends from the descending thoracic aorta to the entire abdominal aorta — the most extensive type. Carries highest risk because it involves sacrifice of the greatest length of intercostal arteries, including T8–12 which supply the artery of Adamkiewicz → spinal cord ischaemia (paraplegia) risk up to 10–20%.
What is REBOA and in which zone is it deployed for a ruptured AAA? flip ↓
REBOA = Resuscitative Endovascular Balloon Occlusion of the Aorta. An endovascular balloon inflated in the aorta to achieve proximal haemostasis. Zone III (infradiaphragmatic aorta, L1–L4) is used for abdominal haemorrhage including rAAA. Zone I (descending thoracic) is used for thoracic/junctional haemorrhage.

Question 1 of 5A 67-year-old man is found on NHS AAA screening to have an aortic diameter of 4.8 cm. He is asymptomatic. What is the most appropriate management?
A. Discharge — below repair threshold
B. Refer for immediate elective repair
C. 3-monthly ultrasound surveillance
D. Annual ultrasound surveillance
NHS screening thresholds: 4.5–5.4 cm → 3-monthly surveillance. Repair is indicated at ≥5.5 cm (men) or ≥5.0 cm (women), or if expansion >1 cm/year, or if symptomatic. Annual surveillance applies to 3–4.4 cm. This patient at 4.8 cm needs 3-monthly follow-up.
Question 2 of 5During open infrarenal AAA repair, you open the posterior peritoneum over the aorta. What is the key landmark that identifies the proximal infrarenal neck?
A. Superior mesenteric artery
B. Left renal vein crossing anteriorly
C. Third part of the duodenum
D. Inferior mesenteric artery origin
The left renal vein crosses ANTERIORLY over the aorta at L1/L2 and is the primary surgical landmark for the infrarenal neck. The proximal clamp is placed just below (or occasionally just above) this vein. The duodenum is an anterior relation that must be reflected, but the left renal vein is the definitive landmark for the infrarenal neck.
Question 3 of 5A patient undergoes EVAR for a 6 cm infrarenal AAA. One month post-procedure, CT angiography shows the aneurysm sac has increased in size by 8 mm. There is a small blush of contrast within the sac not connected to the main graft limbs, appearing to arise from a lumbar artery. What type of endoleak is this?
A. Type I endoleak
B. Type II endoleak
C. Type III endoleak
D. Type IV endoleak
Type II endoleak = retrograde filling from branch vessels (IMA, lumbar arteries). This is the most common type. However, this patient has sac ENLARGEMENT (>5 mm), which mandates intervention (coil embolisation of the feeding vessel). Type I = seal zone failure (proximal/distal attachment). Type III = graft fabric/junction failure. Type IV = porosity of older grafts (rare now).
Question 4 of 5A CT scan describes the IMA as "enlarged and tortuous with an arc of Riolan connecting SMA to IMA territories." What does this finding indicate?
A. Normal variant of no clinical significance
B. AAA with IMA involvement
C. Significant stenosis of either the SMA or IMA has developed, and collateral flow has developed
D. Mesenteric ischaemia is imminent
A prominent arc of Riolan (meandering mesenteric artery) is a collateral pathway that develops when either the SMA or IMA is significantly stenosed/occluded. It is an important surgical finding: during AAA repair, ligating the IMA in the presence of a prominent arc of Riolan is high risk — the colon depends on this collateral. The IMA should be reimplanted or the adequacy of collateral flow must be confirmed by back-pressure measurement.
Question 5 of 5A 70-year-old man presents with sudden severe back pain, a pulsatile abdominal mass, and a systolic BP of 80 mmHg. He is taken to theatre. What is the most important first step in operative management?
A. Open the abdomen and expose the bifurcation first
B. Gain proximal aortic control before opening the haematoma
C. Perform on-table angiography to confirm diagnosis
D. Clamp the common femoral arteries bilaterally
Proximal aortic control BEFORE entering the retroperitoneal haematoma is the cardinal principle of rAAA repair. Releasing the tamponade before having control causes exsanguinating haemorrhage. Control is achieved at the diaphragmatic hiatus (manual or clamp) or with an endovascular balloon (REBOA Zone III). Then the infrarenal neck is dissected for definitive clamp placement under controlled conditions.
