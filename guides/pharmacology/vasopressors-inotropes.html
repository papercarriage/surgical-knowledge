Reading a mcg/kg/min Order
Noradrenaline, adrenaline, and dopamine — what the dose actually means, how to convert it to a pump rate, and why the same number on the pump can mean different things.

What mcg/kg/min means

mcg/kg/min = micrograms of drug, per kilogram of the patient's body weight, per minute. It's a weight-normalised dose, not a pump rate — the pump runs in ml/hr, so you (or the pump's drug library) must convert.

The conversion formula

mcg/kg/min → ml/hr

Rate (ml/hr) = Dose (mcg/kg/min) × Weight (kg) × 60 ÷ Concentration (mcg/ml)

The ×60 converts "per minute" into "per hour," since infusion pumps run in ml/hr.

Worked example — 0.04 mcg/kg/min noradrenaline

Patient 70kg, bag made up as 4mg in 50ml.

  • Concentration: 4mg = 4000mcg ÷ 50ml = 80 mcg/ml
  • Dose per minute: 0.04 × 70 = 2.8 mcg/min
  • Dose per hour: 2.8 × 60 = 168 mcg/hr
  • Pump rate: 168 ÷ 80 = 2.1 ml/hr

Same dose on an 8mg/50ml bag (160 mcg/ml) instead gives 168 ÷ 160 = 1.05 ml/hr — the ml/hr number on the pump is meaningless without knowing the bag concentration. Always sanity-check the mcg/kg/min dose itself, not just what the pump displays.

Practical habit

When you see an infusion rate on a drug chart, reverse the formula to check the mcg/kg/min makes physiological sense for that patient. Decimal-point errors in this conversion are a classic, dangerous prescribing error.

A regional prescribing quirk

Some units prescribe noradrenaline as mcg/min or mg/hour rather than weight-based mcg/kg/min, because weight-based dosing correlates poorly with actual haemodynamic response for this drug, and non-weight-based dosing avoids a common error source (wrong weight entered → wrong rate). Always check which convention your unit uses before doing mental maths — don't assume.

⚠️ Exam Trap

Don't confuse the number on the infusion pump (ml/hr) with the prescribed dose (mcg/kg/min) — they are related only through the bag concentration and the patient's weight. A "correct-looking" pump rate can still be a 10x dosing error if the wrong concentration was assumed.

The Three Drugs — Receptor Pharmacology & Dosing
Same broad drug class, very different dose-response behaviour.

Noradrenaline (norepinephrine)

ReceptorsPotent α1 agonist (vasoconstriction) + mild β1 (inotropy/chronotropy)
Typical dose0.01–0.5 mcg/kg/min (titrated higher in refractory shock)
Primary roleFirst-line vasopressor in septic shock — target MAP ≥65mmHg

Dose-response is fairly linear — vasoconstriction (and MAP/SVR) rises proportionally with dose, without the sharp receptor "tiers" seen with dopamine.

Adrenaline (epinephrine)

Receptorsα1, β1, β2 — genuinely dose-dependent
Typical infusion dose0.01–0.5 mcg/kg/min (second-line vasopressor/inotrope)
Also used forAnaphylaxis (IM 0.5mg adult — different route/purpose); cardiac arrest (IV 1mg boluses q3–5min)
Dose (approx.)Dominant receptorNet effect
<0.02–0.05 mcg/kg/minβ (β1 + β2)↑ contractility & HR; β2 vasodilation can lower SVR
>0.1–0.2 mcg/kg/minα1Vasoconstriction dominates

Dopamine

DoseReceptor dominanceEffect
1–5 mcg/kg/min ("low/renal dose")D1 (dopaminergic)Renal & splanchnic vasodilation
5–10 mcg/kg/min ("intermediate/β dose")β1↑ contractility & heart rate
>10 mcg/kg/min ("high/α dose")α1Vasoconstriction
⚠️ Exam Trap

"Renal-dose dopamine" is a debunked concept. Despite D1-mediated renal vasodilation at low doses, no trial has shown genuine renal-protective or outcome benefit — a classic "sounds physiologically plausible but doesn't survive contact with evidence" teaching point, tested precisely because it's counterintuitive.

Dopamine has fallen out of first-line use since the SOAP II trial found it associated with more arrhythmias than noradrenaline, with a mortality signal specifically in the cardiogenic shock subgroup.

Surgical Relevance
Where these drugs actually show up on your ward round and in theatre.

Access

  • Central venous access is preferred once vasopressor doses escalate — extravasation of a potent vasoconstrictor causes severe local tissue ischaemia/necrosis.
  • Many units now tolerate short-term peripheral infusion of low-dose noradrenaline (via a large, proximal, well-sited cannula) while central access is being arranged — check your unit's protocol rather than assuming either way.
  • Extravasation management: stop the infusion, aspirate what you can from the cannula, and the classic answer is local infiltration with phentolamine (an α-blocker) to reverse the vasoconstriction and limit tissue injury.

Monitoring

Patients on a vasopressor/inotrope infusion should have continuous invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring (arterial line) rather than relying on intermittent non-invasive cuff readings — titration decisions are made in real time against a MAP target.

Where you'll encounter this as a surgical trainee

  • Intra-operative hypotension: anaesthetic team may start a vasopressor for anaesthetic-induced vasodilation or blood loss — understanding the drug helps you interpret the haemodynamic trend during a difficult case.
  • Post-operative septic shock (e.g. anastomotic leak, intra-abdominal collection): noradrenaline is first-line once fluid resuscitation alone isn't maintaining MAP — recognising the need to escalate (and call for senior/ICU input) is a core ward-round skill.
  • Weaning: vasopressors are weaned as the underlying cause is treated (source control, resolving sepsis) — a patient who can't be weaned, or who needs escalating doses, is a red flag for ongoing/undrained sepsis and should prompt a search for a surgical source, not just pharmacological escalation.
Practical pearl

If a post-operative patient's lactate is rising while on an adrenaline infusion, don't assume it means worsening perfusion by default — adrenaline itself raises lactate via β2-mediated glycolysis. Interpret lactate trends on adrenaline alongside other perfusion markers (urine output, capillary refill, base excess), not lactate in isolation.
