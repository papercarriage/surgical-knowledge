Classification & Pathophysiology
A common on-call presentation with one critical mimic you must never miss.

Types

TypeTypical patientMechanism
Lactational mastitisBreastfeeding womenMilk stasis → bacterial entry via cracked nipple, most often Staphylococcus aureus
Non-lactational mastitisNon-breastfeeding womenOften linked to periductal mastitis / duct ectasia, more common in smokers
Periductal mastitisYounger, smokersPeri-areolar inflammation associated with duct ectasia — prone to recurrence

Organisms

Staphylococcus aureus is the dominant organism across most presentations; anaerobes are more relevant in periductal mastitis/recurrent peri-areolar sepsis.

⚠️ Exam Trap

Mastitis or a "breast infection" that doesn't resolve with appropriate antibiotics and drainage must be biopsied to exclude inflammatory breast cancer. Inflammatory breast cancer presents with erythema, warmth, and skin changes (peau d'orange) that can look identical to infection — this is one of the most dangerous diagnostic traps in breast surgery, precisely because the default assumption is "infection."

Management Ladder
Escalation depends on whether an abscess has formed, and how large/complex it is.
PresentationManagement
Cellulitis without a collectionOral antibiotics (cover S. aureus), continue breastfeeding/expressing if lactational — this helps clear milk stasis and is not contraindicated
Small abscessUltrasound-guided needle aspiration ± antibiotics, often repeated if reaccumulation occurs
Large or multiloculated abscessFormal incision and drainage, sometimes with loop drain placement or packing
Recurrent periductal mastitisMay need duct excision (Hadfield's procedure or total duct excision) once acute infection settles
Key principle

The shift over recent decades has been away from routine formal incision and drainage toward ultrasound-guided aspiration where possible — better cosmetic outcome, less disruption to breastfeeding, and often equally effective for straightforward abscesses.

Surgical Relevance
The on-call decision points, and the safety-netting that matters most.

Assessment on-call

  • Examine for a discrete fluctuant collection vs diffuse cellulitis — this determines whether aspiration/drainage is needed or antibiotics alone will suffice.
  • Ultrasound is invaluable both to confirm/characterise a collection and to guide aspiration.
  • Ask about breastfeeding status and duration of symptoms — relevant to both diagnosis and reassurance about continuing to feed.

Safety-netting — the single most important habit

Any patient treated for "mastitis" or a "breast abscess" who does not fully resolve, or who has atypical features (older age, non-lactational, persistent skin changes despite treatment), needs a clear follow-up plan with imaging and biopsy if there is any doubt — this is the safety net that catches inflammatory breast cancer before it's missed as "recurrent infection."

Viva pearl

If asked "how would you distinguish mastitis from inflammatory breast cancer," the expected answer isn't a single sign — it's the trajectory: mastitis should respond to appropriate treatment within days; failure to resolve, or recurrence without a clear precipitant, is the trigger for biopsy.
