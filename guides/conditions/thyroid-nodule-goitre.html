Thyroid Nodule & Goitre

Thyroid nodules are common and mostly benign. The skill is to stratify risk with ultrasound and FNAC, recognise the indeterminate group, and know when a goitre needs an operation.

Thyroid Nodule

Epidemiology and Risk

• Palpable nodules in about 5%; ultrasound finds them in 20–70%. Malignancy in about 5–15%

• Red flags: hard, fixed nodule; rapid growth; new hoarseness; cervical nodes; age under 20 or over 60; male sex; head and neck radiation; family history of medullary cancer or MEN2

Work-up Pathway

• 1. TSH (and fT4). If suppressed, consider a radionuclide scan; hot nodules are rarely malignant

• 2. Neck ultrasound with BTA U1–U5 grading (microcalcification, hypoechogenicity, irregular margins, taller than wide, vascularity); assess nodes

• 3. FNAC (US-guided) for U3 and above, reported as Thy1–Thy5

• 4. Vocal cord check if voice change, previous neck surgery or planned surgery

Goitre

Types and Causes

• Diffuse: iodine deficiency, Graves', Hashimoto's, physiological

• Multinodular (MNG): most common cause of goitre in iodine-sufficient areas; may become toxic (Plummer's)

• Retrosternal: extends below the thoracic inlet, nearly always from the lower pole

• Solitary toxic adenoma, thyroiditis, malignancy

⚠️ Exam Trap

Follicular neoplasm (Thy3f) cannot be separated from follicular carcinoma on FNAC, because carcinoma needs capsular or vascular invasion on histology. It needs diagnostic lobectomy, not repeat FNAC alone.

Indications for Surgery in Goitre

• Compressive symptoms (dyspnoea, stridor, dysphagia), retrosternal extension, suspicion or confirmation of malignancy, toxic MNG failing medical therapy, cosmesis

• Pemberton's sign (facial plethora, venous distension on raising both arms) suggests thoracic inlet obstruction

• Imaging: CT neck and chest for retrosternal extent and tracheal deviation; flow-volume loop if respiratory symptoms

⚠️ Exam Trap

A hot nodule on scintigraphy is almost never malignant, so it does not need FNAC. A retrosternal goitre is nearly always removed through a cervical incision (blood supply is from neck vessels); sternotomy is rarely needed.

Surgical Relevance

Operative choices and perioperative issues.

Choice of Operation

• Hemithyroidectomy (lobectomy): solitary nodule, Thy3f, low-risk small cancer, unilateral disease; avoids lifelong levothyroxine in most

• Total thyroidectomy: MNG with bilateral disease, compressive goitre, Thy4/5 with high-risk features, toxic MNG

• Completion thyroidectomy if final histology shows cancer needing total removal

Retrosternal Goitre

• Pre-op: CT; airway assessment with anaesthetist (tracheal compression, difficult intubation); consider a flexible laryngoscopy

• Deliver the goitre from below upwards with finger dissection after ligating the superior pole and mobilising the lobe; identify the RLN before delivery

• Beware: tracheomalacia after long-standing compression; vessels (thyroidea ima) low in the neck

Complications to Counsel

• RLN palsy, hypocalcaemia (total), haematoma, scar, need for levothyroxine (all after total, some after lobectomy)

⚠️ Exam Trap

A lobectomy specimen showing carcinoma may need completion thyroidectomy depending on size, invasion and nodes. Discuss this at the MDT, and consent for the possibility before the first operation.

Initial investigations for a thyroid nodule? flip ↓
TSH, neck ultrasound (with nodes), then US-guided FNAC if the nodule is U3 or above. Scan only if TSH is suppressed.
Red flags for malignancy in a thyroid nodule? flip ↓
Hard fixed nodule, rapid growth, hoarseness, cervical nodes, age under 20 or over 60, male, radiation exposure, family history of MTC/MEN2.
What does Thy3f mean and what is the management? flip ↓
Follicular lesion, neoplasm possible (about 15–30% malignant). Cytology cannot tell adenoma from carcinoma; diagnostic lobectomy after MDT.
What does Thy4 mean? flip ↓
Suspicious of malignancy (about 60–75% malignant). Surgery after MDT discussion.
Why can FNAC not diagnose follicular carcinoma? flip ↓
Diagnosis needs capsular or vascular invasion, which requires histology of the whole lesion.
Sonographic features suggesting malignancy? flip ↓
Microcalcifications, solid hypoechoic nodule, irregular margins, taller-than-wide shape, intranodular vascularity, suspicious nodes.
What is Pemberton's sign? flip ↓
Facial flushing and venous distension when both arms are raised, due to thoracic inlet obstruction by a retrosternal goitre.
Indications for surgery in goitre? flip ↓
Compressive symptoms, retrosternal extension, malignancy or suspicion of it, toxic goitre not controlled medically, cosmesis.
Usual approach for retrosternal goitre? flip ↓
Cervical collar incision in over 90%; sternotomy only for the minority (ectopic/posterior mediastinal, recurrent, malignant).
Is a hot nodule likely to be malignant? flip ↓
No. Hot nodules are almost never malignant and do not need FNAC.
Commonest cause of goitre in iodine-sufficient regions? flip ↓
Multinodular goitre (may become toxic: Plummer's disease).
What is the operation for a solitary Thy3f nodule? flip ↓
Hemithyroidectomy (lobectomy). Completion thyroidectomy only if final histology shows high-risk cancer.

Question 1 of 5A 45-year-old woman has a 2 cm solitary thyroid nodule, normal TSH, U3 ultrasound. FNAC is Thy3f. What is the best next step?
A. A. Discharge with annual review
B. B. Repeat FNAC and wait
C. C. Diagnostic hemithyroidectomy after MDT discussion
D. D. Radioiodine ablation
Thy3f (follicular lesion) cannot be classified as adenoma or carcinoma on cytology. Diagnostic lobectomy provides histology and, in most, final treatment.
Question 2 of 5Which finding on thyroid scintigraphy makes malignancy least likely?
A. A. A cold nodule
B. B. A single hot nodule
C. C. A cold nodule with calcification
D. D. A nodule with a halo and irregular margins
Hyperfunctioning (hot) nodules are rarely malignant. Cold nodules need ultrasound and FNAC.
Question 3 of 5A patient with a long-standing goitre has dyspnoea and facial plethora on raising both arms. What does this indicate?
A. A. Pemberton's sign: thoracic inlet obstruction
B. B. Chvostek's sign
C. C. Graves' orbitopathy
D. D. Superior vena cava stent block
Pemberton's sign is facial congestion, stridor or distended neck veins on elevating both arms, from thoracic inlet obstruction by a retrosternal goitre. CT of neck and chest and an airway plan are needed.
Question 4 of 5Which feature makes a nodule most worrying on history?
A. A. Female sex
B. B. Pregnancy
C. C. Gradual growth over 10 years
D. D. Prior childhood neck irradiation and new hoarseness
Radiation exposure and hoarseness (possible RLN invasion) are strong red flags. Hoarseness should prompt laryngoscopy and usually surgical planning.
Question 5 of 5What is the usual surgical approach for a retrosternal goitre?
A. A. Median sternotomy
B. B. Left thoracotomy
C. C. Right VATS
D. D. Cervical collar incision
Most retrosternal goitres derive their blood supply from the neck and can be delivered through a cervical incision. Sternotomy is reserved for a minority.
