Primary Hyperparathyroidism

PHPT is the commonest cause of outpatient hypercalcaemia and a classic surgical endocrine problem. Know the pathology, the diagnostic algorithm, the indications for surgery and why localisation does not make the diagnosis.

Pathology

CauseFrequencyNotes
Single adenoma~85%Focused surgery possible
Multiglandular hyperplasia~10–15%MEN1, MEN2A, familial; needs subtotal or total with autotransplant
Double adenoma~2–5%Easily missed with focused approach
Parathyroid carcinomaUnder 1%Very high Ca (above 3.5), palpable mass, invade locally
Presentation

• Mostly asymptomatic. "Stones, bones, abdominal groans and psychic moans"

• Renal stones, nephrocalcinosis, osteoporosis, osteitis fibrosa cystica (brown tumours, subperiosteal resorption in phalanges, salt-and-pepper skull), peptic ulcer, pancreatitis, depression, hypertension

• Typically postmenopausal women; also associated with MEN1 and MEN2A

⚠️ Exam Trap

Biochemistry makes the diagnosis; imaging only localises. Do not request sestamibi to decide whether someone has PHPT. Exclude familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH) with a 24-hour urine calcium or calcium/creatinine clearance ratio before surgery.

Key Facts & Numbers

Diagnosis, localisation and indications for surgery.

Diagnosis

• High calcium + high or inappropriately normal PTH, low phosphate, raised ALP in bone disease

• Check vitamin D (replete if deficient), eGFR, 24-hour urinary calcium (FHH), DEXA (distal 1/3 radius), renal imaging for stones

Localisation

• Neck ultrasound; 99mTc-sestamibi SPECT/CT; 4D-CT or choline PET for difficult cases

• Concordant imaging allows focused (minimally invasive) parathyroidectomy; discordant or negative imaging means bilateral neck exploration by an experienced surgeon

Indications for Surgery (Fourth International Workshop)

• Symptomatic disease (stones, fragility fracture, neuropsychiatric)

• Asymptomatic: age under 50; serum calcium more than 0.25 mmol/L above ULN; T-score of −2.5 or lower or vertebral fracture; eGFR below 60; nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis

• Cinacalcet or bisphosphonate for those unfit or declining surgery

Operative Strategy

ScenarioOperation
Single concordant adenomaFocused parathyroidectomy ± IOPTH
Negative / discordant imagingBilateral neck exploration, identify all four glands
Multiglandular (MEN1)3.5-gland (subtotal) or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplant ± thymectomy
CarcinomaEn bloc excision including ipsilateral thyroid lobe; avoid capsule breach
⚠️ Exam Trap

MEN1 (menin; parathyroid, pituitary, pancreas) has multiglandular hyperplasia, so a focused single-gland excision fails. Always think of the "3Ps". A very high calcium with a palpable mass should raise suspicion of parathyroid carcinoma.

Surgical Relevance

The operation, ectopic glands and the failures.

Operative Principles

• Bilateral exploration: expose both sides, inspect the four expected positions; recall that superior glands are dorsal to the RLN and inferior are ventral

• Frozen section or IOPTH: more than 50% fall at 10 minutes from the highest pre-excision value suggests cure

• Remove the abnormal gland intact; keep the capsule; avoid spillage (parathyromatosis)

• Mark remaining normal glands; autotransplant if all removed

Ectopic Glands (Where to Look)

• Intrathyroid, thyrothymic ligament/thymus, carotid sheath, retro-oesophageal/tracheo-oesophageal groove, mediastinum, undescended near carotid bifurcation

• Persistent disease (calcium high within 6 months): reassess biochemistry, re-image, consider re-operation in a high-volume centre

Post-operative

• Check calcium and PTH at day 1; watch for hungry bone syndrome (low Ca, PO4, Mg); transient hypocalcaemia with supplements; RLN palsy; haematoma

• Recurrent disease: after 6 months of normocalcaemia

Clinical Pearl

Neck haematoma after parathyroid surgery is managed exactly as after thyroid surgery: bedside decompression first.

Biochemistry of PHPT? flip ↓

Radiological bone signs of hyperparathyroidism? flip ↓
Subperiosteal resorption of phalanges, salt-and-pepper skull, brown tumours, osteitis fibrosa cystica.
First-line localisation tests? flip ↓
Neck ultrasound and 99mTc-sestamibi SPECT/CT. 4D-CT or choline PET when negative.
Indications for surgery in asymptomatic PHPT? flip ↓
Age under 50; Ca more than 0.25 mmol/L above ULN; T-score ≤ −2.5 or fracture; eGFR below 60; stones/nephrocalcinosis.
When is focused parathyroidectomy appropriate? flip ↓
Single adenoma with concordant imaging (US + sestamibi), using IOPTH to confirm cure.
IOPTH criterion for cure? flip ↓
More than a 50% fall from the highest pre-excision value at 10 minutes (Miami criterion).
Operation for MEN1-related PHPT? flip ↓
Multiglandular: subtotal (3.5-gland) or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplant ± transcervical thymectomy.
Features of parathyroid carcinoma? flip ↓
Very high calcium (above 3.5 mmol/L), palpable neck mass, nodal disease; treat with en bloc resection.
Where are ectopic inferior and superior glands found? flip ↓
Inferior: thymus/thyrothymic ligament, mediastinum. Superior: retro-oesophageal, tracheo-oesophageal groove, posterior mediastinum.
Define persistent vs recurrent PHPT. flip ↓
Persistent: hypercalcaemia within 6 months of surgery. Recurrent: after 6 months of normocalcaemia.

Question 1 of 5A 62-year-old woman has calcium 2.9 mmol/L, phosphate 0.7 mmol/L and PTH 9 pmol/L. 24-hour urinary calcium is normal. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. A. Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia
B. B. Primary hyperparathyroidism
C. C. Vitamin D toxicity
D. D. Malignancy-related hypercalcaemia
High calcium, low phosphate and raised PTH indicate PHPT. FHH would show a low calcium/creatinine clearance ratio (below 0.01) and normal-range PTH.
Question 2 of 5Which statement about localisation in PHPT is correct?
A. A. A positive sestamibi scan confirms the diagnosis
B. B. A negative scan excludes the diagnosis
C. C. Imaging is used after biochemical diagnosis to plan the operation
D. D. CT is the first-line test
Biochemistry diagnoses PHPT. Imaging (ultrasound and sestamibi SPECT/CT) plans the surgical approach, allowing focused surgery in concordant cases.
Question 3 of 5A 28-year-old with PHPT also has a pituitary tumour and a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour. What operation is appropriate for the parathyroids?
A. A. Focused single-gland excision
B. B. Observation
C. C. Cinacalcet only
D. D. Subtotal or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplant
This is MEN1, with multiglandular hyperplasia. Single-gland surgery will fail; subtotal (3.5-gland) or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation is performed.
Question 4 of 5Five days after parathyroidectomy for severe disease with brown tumours, calcium 1.6 mmol/L, phosphate 0.5, magnesium 0.5, ALP markedly raised. What is this?
A. A. Hypoparathyroidism
B. B. Hungry bone syndrome
C. C. Pseudohypoparathyroidism
D. D. Vitamin D toxicity
Low Ca, low PO4 and low Mg with raised ALP after parathyroidectomy is hungry bone syndrome. In hypoparathyroidism phosphate would be high.
Question 5 of 5Which is an accepted indication for surgery in asymptomatic PHPT?
A. A. Age 65, calcium within 0.1 mmol/L of ULN, normal DEXA
B. B. Age 45
C. C. A positive sestamibi scan alone
D. D. Mild fatigue only
Age under 50 is an indication. Other criteria: calcium more than 0.25 mmol/L above ULN, T-score ≤ −2.5 or fracture, eGFR below 60, stones/nephrocalcinosis.
