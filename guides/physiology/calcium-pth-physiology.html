Calcium & PTH Physiology

Calcium handling is the physiology behind parathyroid surgery and the commonest metabolic complication of thyroidectomy. Learn the feedback loop, the biochemical patterns that separate the causes, and how to treat the emergencies.

Calcium in Blood

Distribution and Correction

• Total calcium 2.2–2.6 mmol/L; ionised 1.1–1.3 mmol/L is the active fraction

• About 50% ionised, 40% albumin-bound, 10% complexed (citrate, phosphate)

• Corrected Ca = measured Ca + 0.02 × (40 − albumin g/L)

• Alkalosis (hyperventilation) lowers ionised calcium and causes tetany; acidosis raises ionised calcium

The Hormones

PTH

• 84-amino-acid peptide from chief cells. Half-life 3–5 minutes. Released when the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) senses low calcium

• Bone: osteoblasts express RANKL, activating osteoclasts, releasing calcium and phosphate

• Kidney: increases distal tubular calcium reabsorption, reduces proximal phosphate reabsorption (phosphaturia), activates 1α-hydroxylase

• Gut: indirect, via 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D

Vitamin D

• Skin 7-dehydrocholesterol (UVB) to cholecalciferol, then liver 25-hydroxylation, then kidney 1α-hydroxylation to calcitriol (active)

• Calcitriol increases intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption and supports bone mineralisation

• The stored form, 25-OH vitamin D, is what is measured clinically

Calcitonin and Magnesium

• Calcitonin: C cells; lowers calcium, minor in humans. Tumour marker for medullary thyroid cancer

• Magnesium is needed for PTH secretion and action; hypomagnesaemia causes refractory hypocalcaemia

⚠️ Exam Trap

PTH increases calcium and decreases phosphate (phosphaturia). Hypoparathyroidism gives low calcium with high phosphate. Do not confuse with vitamin D deficiency (low phosphate, raised PTH, raised ALP).

Clinical Features

Hypercalcaemia

• "Stones, bones, groans and psychic moans"; polyuria, dehydration, constipation, short QT

• First-line: IV 0.9% saline; then bisphosphonate (zoledronic acid); calcitonin for rapid effect; steroids for myeloma, sarcoid, vitamin D toxicity; loop diuretic only after rehydration

Hypocalcaemia

• Perioral tingling, paraesthesia, Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs, tetany, seizures, long QT

• Acute severe: 10 mL 10% calcium gluconate IV over 10 minutes with ECG monitoring, then infusion; correct magnesium

• Oral: calcium plus alfacalcidol or calcitriol for hypoparathyroidism

⚠️ Exam Trap

Always correct calcium for albumin before acting. In acute hypocalcaemia, check magnesium: low Mg stops PTH working and calcium will not stay corrected. Loop diuretics in dehydrated hypercalcaemia worsen things; fluids first.

Surgical Relevance

Where calcium physiology meets the operating table.

Post-thyroidectomy Hypocalcaemia

• Causes: parathyroid devascularisation or removal, and transient stunning. Peaks around 24–72 hours

• Risk: total thyroidectomy, central neck dissection, Graves', retrosternal goitre, inadvertent excision

• Many units measure PTH on day 0 or 1 and use a threshold to guide early calcium supplementation and discharge

• Permanent hypoparathyroidism: persisting beyond about 6–12 months

Hungry Bone Syndrome

• After parathyroidectomy in severe disease: low Ca, low PO4, low Mg and high ALP as bone avidly takes up minerals

• Needs prolonged high-dose calcium and vitamin D; distinguish from hypoparathyroidism (which has a high phosphate)

Intra-operative PTH (IOPTH)

• Uses the short half-life of PTH: sample pre-excision and at 10 minutes after excision

• Common criterion (Miami): a fall of more than 50% from the highest pre-excision value into the normal range suggests cure

Clinical Pearl

Tetany after thyroidectomy: treat the patient, not the number. Symptoms or QT prolongation justify IV calcium gluconate even before the lab result returns.

Question 1 of 5A patient has serum calcium 3.1 mmol/L, phosphate 0.6 mmol/L and PTH 12 pmol/L (raised). Which diagnosis is most likely?
A. A. Hypoparathyroidism
B. B. Vitamin D deficiency
C. C. Malignancy with bone metastases
D. D. Primary hyperparathyroidism
High calcium, low phosphate and a raised PTH point to primary hyperparathyroidism. Malignancy suppresses PTH. Hypoparathyroidism and vitamin D deficiency both give low or low-normal calcium.
Question 2 of 5A patient develops perioral tingling 36 hours after total thyroidectomy. Corrected calcium is 1.85 mmol/L, and Trousseau's sign is positive. What is the next step?
A. A. IV calcium gluconate with ECG monitoring, and check magnesium
B. B. Oral vitamin D only
C. C. Furosemide
D. D. Observe overnight
Symptomatic hypocalcaemia is treated with IV calcium gluconate (10 mL of 10% over 10 minutes) with ECG monitoring. Check and correct magnesium. Oral calcium and vitamin D follow.
Question 3 of 5What is the effect of PTH on the kidney?
A. A. Increases phosphate reabsorption and decreases calcium reabsorption
B. B. Increases calcium reabsorption and increases phosphate excretion
C. C. Decreases both calcium and phosphate reabsorption
D. D. Inhibits 1α-hydroxylase
PTH increases distal tubular calcium reabsorption, inhibits proximal tubular phosphate reabsorption (phosphaturia), and stimulates 1α-hydroxylase.
Question 4 of 5A dehydrated patient has calcium 3.4 mmol/L. What is the first treatment?
A. A. Zoledronic acid immediately
B. B. Furosemide
C. C. IV 0.9% saline
D. D. Oral phosphate
Volume depletion worsens hypercalcaemia by impairing renal calcium excretion. Rehydrate with IV 0.9% saline first; bisphosphonates take days to act and are given after fluids. Loop diuretics only once euvolaemic.
Question 5 of 5Five days after parathyroidectomy for severe hyperparathyroidism, calcium, phosphate and magnesium are all low and ALP is high. What is the diagnosis?
A. A. Hungry bone syndrome
B. B. Permanent hypoparathyroidism
C. C. Vitamin D toxicity
D. D. Persistent hyperparathyroidism
Hungry bone syndrome: rapid skeletal uptake of minerals after PTH excess is removed, giving low Ca, PO4 and Mg with raised ALP. Hypoparathyroidism has a high phosphate.
