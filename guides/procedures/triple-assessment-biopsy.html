The Triple Assessment Principle
No breast lesion is diagnosed on any single arm alone — clinical, radiological, and pathological findings must be concordant before a management plan is made.

The three arms

ArmWhat it contributes
Clinical assessmentHistory, examination — location, consistency, mobility, skin/nipple changes, axillary/nodal exam
ImagingMammography (calcifications, masses), ultrasound (cystic vs solid, guides biopsy), MRI in selected cases (dense breasts, high-risk screening, extent of disease assessment)
PathologyCore biopsy or FNA — the tissue diagnosis
The core concept

A benign-looking biopsy result in the face of a clinically or radiologically suspicious lesion is discordant — it does not settle the question. Discordance triggers further action (repeat biopsy, excision biopsy), not reassurance.

Core Biopsy vs FNA, and Image Guidance
Know which technique is used for which scenario, and why core biopsy is now the default.

Core biopsy vs fine needle aspiration (FNA)

FeatureCore biopsyFNA
Sample typeHistology (preserves architecture)Cytology only (cells, no architecture)
Can assess invasion (in situ vs invasive)?YesNo
Receptor status testing possible?YesGenerally no
Current roleDefault for a new suspicious solid lesionNiche use — e.g. simple cyst aspiration, rapid assessment of a clinically obvious node

Image guidance

  • Ultrasound-guided: used when the lesion is visible on ultrasound — most solid masses.
  • Stereotactic-guided: used for lesions only visible on mammography, typically calcifications not associated with a discrete ultrasound-visible mass.
  • Vacuum-assisted biopsy: larger-volume sampling, often used for calcifications to improve diagnostic yield, and sometimes therapeutically to excise small benign lesions.

Core biopsy B-classification

CategoryMeaning
B1Normal tissue
B2Benign
B3Uncertain malignant potential
B4Suspicious of malignancy
B5Malignant (B5a in situ, B5b invasive)
Surgical Relevance
What happens when the three arms disagree, and how this shapes the pathway to treatment.

Managing discordance

  • If clinical/imaging findings are suspicious but core biopsy returns benign (B2) — the biopsy result cannot be trusted in isolation; repeat biopsy or proceed to excision biopsy is warranted.
  • B3 lesions (uncertain malignant potential — e.g. atypical ductal hyperplasia, some papillary lesions) typically require further excision (often vacuum-assisted or surgical) rather than reassurance, given the risk of an associated malignancy elsewhere in the lesion.

Why this matters for planning surgery

Everything downstream — breast-conserving surgery vs mastectomy, need for axillary staging, eligibility for neoadjuvant therapy — depends on having a confirmed, concordant tissue diagnosis with receptor status established before treatment planning begins.

⚠️ Exam Trap

A "normal" or "benign" core biopsy does not end the workup if the clinical or radiological picture remains suspicious. This is a frequently tested scenario precisely because it's an easy trap to fall into — trusting a reassuring biopsy over a suspicious clinical picture.
