Appendicectomy

A run-through of everything you need for an appendicectomy case: anatomy, diagnosis, consent, the laparoscopic steps, intra-operative curveballs, complications, trial evidence and likely viva questions.

Anatomy

Landmarks and Position

• Base: where the three taeniae coli converge on the caecum. Follow the taeniae when the appendix is hard to find

• McBurney's point: two-thirds of the way from the umbilicus to the ASIS

• Tip position: retrocaecal about 65–75%; pelvic about 20–30%; subcaecal, pre-ileal and post-ileal the rest. Post-ileal is classically atypical and difficult

• Blood supply: appendicular artery, from the ileocolic artery (SMA). It runs in the free edge of the mesoappendix and is an end artery, so gangrene and perforation occur. An accessory (posterior caecal) artery may be present

• Bloodless fold of Treves: the ileocaecal fold, a landmark rather than something to divide

Open Approach

• Incisions: Lanz (skin crease, better cosmesis) or Gridiron (oblique, through McBurney's point)

• Layers: skin, Camper's, Scarpa's, external oblique aponeurosis, internal oblique, transversus (both split along their fibres), transversalis fascia, extraperitoneal fat, peritoneum

• Nerves at risk: iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal. Injury can cause a later direct inguinal hernia

Meckel's Diverticulum: Rule of 2s

• 2% of people, about 2 ft (60 cm) from the ileocaecal valve, about 2 inches long, 2 tissue types (gastric and pancreatic)

• Look for it if the appendix is normal: run about 2 ft of ileum back from the ileocaecal valve

Diagnosis

History and Signs

• Periumbilical visceral pain (T10) migrating to the RIF (parietal); anorexia almost universal; Murphy's sequence is pain, then vomiting

• Rovsing's: pressing the LIF causes RIF pain. Psoas sign: retrocaecal. Obturator sign: pelvic (internal rotation of the flexed hip). Dunphy's: pain on coughing

⚠️ Exam Trap

Pain in the RIF in a woman of reproductive age needs a β-hCG before anything else. A retrocaecal appendix gives a positive psoas sign; a pelvic appendix gives the obturator sign and may cause diarrhoea and urinary symptoms.

Score Use and Imaging

• Score of 7 or more makes appendicitis likely. WSES (Jerusalem 2020) favours the AIR score or the Adult Appendicitis Score over Alvarado

• Ultrasound first in children, young women and pregnancy; CT is the most accurate test in adults; MRI if ultrasound is inconclusive in pregnancy

• Features: diameter over 6 mm, non-compressible, periappendiceal fat stranding, appendicolith

Pre-operative

• Consent: bleeding, infection (SSI, collection), conversion to open, bowel/bladder/vessel injury, port-site hernia, ileus, removal of a normal appendix, unexpected pathology (tumour) needing further surgery, VTE

• Antibiotics: cefazolin plus metronidazole (or co-amoxiclav) within 60 minutes of incision. Organisms: E. coli and Bacteroides fragilis

• Bladder: void or catheterise before the suprapubic port

Evidence

TrialKey result
CODA (NEJM 2020)Antibiotics non-inferior to surgery for quality of life. About 30% had appendicectomy by 90 days, about 40% if an appendicolith was present
APPAC (JAMA)Recurrence after antibiotics about 27% at 1 year and about 39% at 5 years
WSES 2020Antibiotics alone is an option for selected uncomplicated cases without an appendicolith; appendicectomy remains standard
STOP-ITAbout 4 days of antibiotics is enough after source control in complicated intra-abdominal infection
⚠️ Exam Trap

Uncomplicated appendicitis needs no post-operative antibiotics; complicated (perforated, gangrenous with abscess) needs about 4 days. An appendix mass after day 5 is managed conservatively, and over-40s need colonoscopy or CT to exclude malignancy.

Surgical Relevance

Laparoscopic steps, curveballs, special groups and complications.

Laparoscopic Steps

Set-up

• Position: supine, left arm tucked; surgeon and assistant on the patient's left, monitor on the right; Trendelenburg with left-side-down tilt

• Ports: 10/12 mm umbilical (open Hasson), 5 mm suprapubic, 5 mm LIF. Avoid the inferior epigastric vessels

StepKey points
1. Diagnostic laparoscopyPelvis, right paracolic gutter, subhepatic space; note pus and its location
2. Find the appendixFollow the taeniae to the base; mobilise lateral attachments if retrocaecal
3. Lift the tipGrasp the mesoappendix or tip and tent it up
4. WindowMake a window in the mesoappendix at the base
5. Divide the mesoappendixDiathermy, harmonic scalpel, clips or vascular stapler
6. Secure the baseTwo endoloops proximally (± one distally), or a stapler if the base is inflamed or necrotic
7. Divide and retrieveRemove in a retrieval bag through the 10 mm port
8. Suction and washoutLocal suction is enough; extensive washout is not proven to reduce collections
9. If normalLook for Meckel's, ileitis, gynaecological pathology
10. CloseFascia at the 10 mm port
Clinical Pearl

Leave a stump under 5 mm; a longer stump risks stump appendicitis.

⚠️ Exam Trap

If the base is necrotic or perforated, do not endoloop the friable tissue. Staple across healthy caecum. If the caecum itself is involved you may need an ileocaecal resection.

Intra-op Curveballs

Know These

• Normal-looking appendix: usually remove it, then look for Meckel's, terminal ileitis, mesenteric adenitis, ovarian torsion/cyst, ectopic pregnancy, PID, caecal diverticulitis

• Crohn's terminal ileitis: remove the appendix if the caecal base is healthy; do not resect ileum

• Mucocele: handle gently, avoid spillage (pseudomyxoma peritonei)

• Neuroendocrine tumour (tip): under 1 cm appendicectomy alone; 1–2 cm right hemicolectomy if high-risk features (base involvement, mesoappendiceal invasion over 3 mm, LVI, higher grade); over 2 cm right hemicolectomy

• Adenocarcinoma / goblet cell adenocarcinoma: right hemicolectomy

• Appendix mass or abscess: antibiotics; percutaneous drain if collection over about 3–4 cm

Special Groups and Complications

Pregnancy and Elderly

• Commonest non-obstetric surgical emergency in pregnancy. The appendix is pushed upwards. Laparoscopy is safe in any trimester: open entry, pneumoperitoneum 10–12 mmHg, left lateral tilt. Perforation raises fetal loss risk

• Elderly: atypical presentation, more perforation, possible underlying neoplasm

Complications

• SSI (commonest). Intra-abdominal or pelvic collection around days 5–7 with swinging fever, diarrhoea or tenesmus: CT then percutaneous drainage

• Stump leak or faecal fistula, ileus, adhesional SBO, port-site hernia, stump appendicitis

• Pylephlebitis: septic portal vein thrombophlebitis causing liver abscesses; rare but a favourite viva question

Likely Viva Questions

• Base and how to find it; blood supply; layers and nerves in open approach; normal appendix at laparoscopy; gangrenous base; why a retrieval bag; post-op antibiotics; day-6 swinging fever; carcinoid by size; Meckel's rule of 2s; mass on day 5; pregnancy differences; antibiotics-only (CODA/APPAC); table tilt; score the Alvarado criteria

Question 1 of 5A 24-year-old has right iliac fossa pain and a positive psoas sign. Which appendix position is most likely?
A. A. Pelvic
B. B. Retrocaecal
C. C. Pre-ileal
D. D. Subcaecal
The psoas sign (pain on hip extension) suggests an inflamed retrocaecal appendix irritating psoas. A pelvic appendix gives the obturator sign.
Question 2 of 5At laparoscopy the appendix base is necrotic and friable. What is the safest way to secure it?
A. A. Two endoloops on the necrotic base
B. B. Simple ligation with absorbable suture
C. C. Staple across healthy caecum
D. D. Leave a long stump and drain
Endoloops cut through necrotic tissue. A linear stapler across healthy caecum gives a secure closure. If caecum is involved an ileocaecal resection may be needed.
Question 3 of 5A 52-year-old presents on day 6 with an RIF mass and low-grade fever. CT shows a phlegmon without a large abscess. What is the best management?
A. A. Immediate open appendicectomy
B. B. Conservative management with antibiotics, then colonoscopy or CT to exclude malignancy
C. C. Immediate right hemicolectomy
D. D. Discharge without follow-up
Late-presenting appendix masses are treated conservatively. Over-40s need colonoscopy or interval imaging to exclude caecal or appendiceal cancer.
Question 4 of 5A 1.4 cm neuroendocrine tumour is found at the appendix tip with mesoappendiceal invasion of 4 mm. What is the recommended treatment?
A. A. No further treatment
B. B. Right hemicolectomy
C. C. Radiotherapy
D. D. Repeat appendicectomy
Tumours of 1–2 cm with high-risk features (mesoappendiceal invasion over 3 mm, base involvement, LVI, higher grade) merit right hemicolectomy. Under 1 cm appendicectomy alone is enough.
Question 5 of 5Which statement about antibiotics after appendicectomy is correct?
A. A. None after uncomplicated appendicitis; about 4 days if complicated
B. B. Always 7 days of IV antibiotics
C. C. Always oral antibiotics for 14 days
D. D. None in any case
After source control, uncomplicated appendicitis needs only prophylaxis. Complicated disease needs a short course of about 4 days (STOP-IT).
