Principles of Endovascular Aneurysm Repair
EVAR (abdominal) and TEVAR (thoracic) exclude an aneurysm from arterial pressure using a stent graft, rather than resecting and replacing the diseased segment.

The core concept

Exclusion, not resection

A covered stent graft is deployed inside the aneurysm sac, creating a new conduit for blood flow. The sac is excluded from arterial pressure and, ideally, shrinks over time. Unlike open repair, the diseased wall is left in situ — success depends entirely on achieving a durable seal at each end of the graft.

Indications

  • AAA (EVAR): diameter ≥5.5cm in men, ≥5.0cm in women; growth >1cm/year; symptomatic (pain, tenderness); ruptured (emergency).
  • Descending TAA (TEVAR): diameter ≥5.5–6.0cm; symptomatic or rapidly growing; also used for complicated type B aortic dissection, penetrating aortic ulcer, traumatic aortic transection, and contained rupture.

Anatomical eligibility — what makes a case "EVAR-suitable"

Planning is done on CT angiography with centerline reconstruction. The device is chosen to match:

  • Proximal landing zone (neck): length ≥10–15mm, minimal angulation, free of significant thrombus/calcification, diameter within the device's sizing range (grafts are oversized ~10–20% relative to native vessel diameter for adequate seal).
  • Distal landing zone: common iliac artery of adequate length and diameter; if the aneurysm extends into the iliacs, the landing zone may need to be extended into the external iliac, which risks sacrificing internal iliac artery flow (see Surgical Relevance).
  • Access vessels: iliofemoral diameter, tortuosity, and calcification must accommodate the delivery sheath (typically 14–24Fr depending on device) — this is the most common reason a case is deemed "endovascular-unsuitable."

Procedure steps (typical bifurcated AAA EVAR)

Sequence

1. Bilateral common femoral access (percutaneous with closure device, or surgical cutdown).
2. Wire access up through the aneurysm into the thoracic aorta on both sides.
3. Main body (with ipsilateral limb) deployed from the larger/primary access, positioned below the lowest renal artery.
4. Contralateral limb cannulated via the contralateral gate and deployed down to the contralateral iliac landing zone.
5. Limb extensions added as needed to reach adequate distal landing zones.
6. Balloon moulding of all overlap zones and the proximal/distal seals.
7. Completion angiogram — check for endoleak, confirm graft patency and renal/visceral vessel patency.
8. Access site closure.

TEVAR-specific considerations

  • Landing zones classified by the Ishimaru zone system (Zone 0 = origin of innominate artery, through Zone 5 in the abdominal aorta) — used to describe proximal seal location and whether arch vessels need covering/revascularising.
  • Left subclavian artery coverage is common when the proximal landing zone requires it (Zone 2) — revascularise (carotid-subclavian bypass or transposition) if the patient has a dominant left vertebral artery, prior CABG using LIMA, or a patent left arm dialysis access, to protect posterior circulation and reduce spinal cord ischaemia risk.
  • Spinal cord ischaemia is the feared TEVAR complication — covering a long segment of descending aorta sacrifices intercostal/segmental arteries feeding the anterior spinal artery. Mitigated by permissive hypertension, staged/limited coverage length, and CSF drainage in high-risk cases.
⚠️ Exam Trap

EVAR/TEVAR trades a lower perioperative mortality and shorter recovery for lifelong surveillance and a persistent risk of endoleak, migration, and reintervention — open repair has a durable, one-time result once healed. This trade-off is a classic viva discussion point, especially in younger, fit patients.

Endoleak classification

TypeSourceUrgency
IAttachment site leak — proximal (Ia) or distal (Ib) seal failureTreat — sac remains under systemic pressure
IIRetrograde flow from a branch vessel (lumbar arteries, IMA most common)Often observed if sac is stable/shrinking; treat if sac grows
IIIJunctional separation between graft components, or a fabric tearTreat — sac under systemic pressure, similar risk to Type I
IVGraft porosity (blood "weeping" through fabric)Usually self-limiting, resolves with coagulation reversal
VEndotension — sac enlargement with no visible leak on imagingInvestigate further; may still need intervention if sac keeps growing
⚠️ Exam Trap

Types I and III both expose the sac to full systemic pressure and mandate treatment. Type II is the most common endoleak overall and is the one most often managed expectantly — don't lump all endoleaks together as "needs fixing."

Adjuncts for complex anatomy

  • EndoAnchors (Heli-FX): helical implants that pin the graft fabric to the aortic wall — used for short/hostile necks or to treat/prevent Type Ia endoleak.
  • Chimney/snorkel technique: a parallel stent placed alongside the main graft to preserve flow to a renal/visceral vessel when the landing zone must extend across its origin — an off-label bridge to fenestrated technology.
  • Fenestrated/branched EVAR (FEVAR/BEVAR): custom-made grafts with fenestrations or side branches for renal/visceral vessels, allowing a higher proximal seal in juxtarenal/pararenal aneurysms.
  • Iliac branch devices: preserve antegrade internal iliac artery flow when the distal landing zone must extend into the external iliac artery.

Spinal cord ischaemia (TEVAR)See Overview — permissive hypertension, CSF drainage in high-risk coverage

Post-procedure surveillance — the lifelong commitment

Unlike open repair, EVAR/TEVAR requires ongoing imaging surveillance (CT angiogram typically at 1 month, then 6–12 months, then annually, or duplex ultrasound protocols in some centres) to detect endoleak, sac growth, or migration before they progress to rupture. This surveillance burden — and the cost/compliance issues it raises — is a standard exam and viva discussion point when comparing EVAR to open repair.

When to convert to open repair

  • Anatomy proves unsuitable intra-procedure (inadequate seal despite adjuncts).
  • Uncontrolled Type I/III endoleak not amenable to further endovascular fixes.
  • Graft infection.
  • Access vessels too hostile to deliver the device safely.
Viva pearl

If asked to compare EVAR and open AAA repair: EVAR has lower 30-day mortality and faster recovery but no long-term survival benefit beyond ~2 years, plus the ongoing surveillance/reintervention burden (landmark trials: EVAR-1, DREAM, OVER). Open repair has higher perioperative morbidity but a more durable, "fix-and-forget" result.
