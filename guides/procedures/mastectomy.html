Types of Mastectomy
The spectrum from complete tissue removal to nipple-preserving approaches, each with specific indications and trade-offs.
TypeWhat's removedNotes
Simple (total) mastectomyBreast tissue, nipple-areolar complex, overlying skinAxilla not addressed unless combined with axillary surgery
Modified radical mastectomyAs above, plus axillary lymph node dissectionReserved for confirmed axillary disease not suitable for SLNB alone
Skin-sparing mastectomyBreast tissue and nipple-areolar complex; overlying skin envelope preservedFacilitates immediate reconstruction with a better cosmetic result
Nipple-sparing mastectomyBreast tissue only; skin envelope and nipple-areolar complex preservedBest cosmetic outcome; requires careful patient selection (see below)

⚠️ Exam Trap

Radical (Halsted) mastectomy is a historical procedure — don't confuse it with modified radical mastectomy (still current practice) when answering exam questions about "radical mastectomy."

Indications & Nipple-Sparing Contraindications
Not every patient is a candidate for the more tissue-preserving approaches.

General indications for mastectomy over BCS

  • Multicentric disease, or extensive disease relative to breast size.
  • Contraindication to adjuvant radiotherapy.
  • Patient preference, including risk-reducing (prophylactic) mastectomy in high-risk patients (e.g. BRCA carriers).
  • Inflammatory breast cancer (mastectomy typically after neoadjuvant systemic therapy, not upfront).

Contraindications to nipple-sparing mastectomy

  • Tumour involvement of, or close proximity to, the nipple-areolar complex.
  • Inflammatory breast cancer.
  • Very large or significantly ptotic breasts, where nipple viability after the procedure is a concern.
  • Paget's disease of the nipple (by definition involves the nipple-areolar complex).
Surgical Relevance
Reconstruction timing and the complications worth anticipating on the ward.

Reconstruction timing

TimingConsideration
ImmediateReconstruction performed in the same operation — better cosmetic and psychological outcomes, but adds complexity and length to the primary cancer operation, and complications can occasionally delay adjuvant treatment
DelayedReconstruction performed as a separate, later procedure — often chosen if post-mastectomy radiotherapy is anticipated, since radiotherapy affects reconstructed tissue (see Reconstruction guide)

Complications to anticipate

  • Seroma — common, usually managed with observation or aspiration.
  • Skin flap necrosis — particularly relevant to skin/nipple-sparing approaches where flap vascularity is more marginal.
  • Altered chest wall sensation — near-universal to some degree, given division of cutaneous nerve branches.
  • Wound infection, haematoma.
Viva pearl

If asked why post-mastectomy radiotherapy might push toward delayed rather than immediate reconstruction, the expected answer is that radiotherapy increases complication rates in reconstructed tissue (especially implant-based), so timing is often deliberately sequenced to avoid irradiating a fresh reconstruction.
