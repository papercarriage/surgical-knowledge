General Principles of Angioplasty
Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) — the endovascular workflow from access to closure, and how wire/sheath/balloon selection changes with lesion complexity.

The core workflow

Every endovascular case follows the same skeleton, regardless of vessel bed:

1 → 6: Access to Closure

1. Access — Seldinger technique (needle → wire → sheath) at a chosen site.
2. Sheath — establishes a stable, haemostatic conduit for wire/catheter exchange.
3. Diagnostic run — catheter + contrast to define lesion length, calcification, run-off.
4. Cross the lesion — guidewire (± support catheter) traverses the stenosis/occlusion.
5. Treat — balloon angioplasty ± stent, guided by the wire left across the lesion.
6. Close — manual compression or closure device; check distal pulses/Doppler.

Access sites

Choice drives everything downstream

The access site determines catheter length, sheath orientation, and how much support you'll have reaching the lesion — decide this before you pick a wire.

  • Common femoral, antegrade (ipsilateral) — best pushability for SFA/popliteal/tibial lesions on that side; needs a high enough puncture below the inguinal ligament but above the femoral bifurcation.
  • Common femoral, retrograde with contralateral crossover — over the aortic bifurcation via a crossover sheath (e.g. Balkan, Destination) — standard for contralateral iliac/SFA disease.
  • Brachial / radial — for iliac occlusions that can't be crossed from below, or as an adjunct for retrograde tibial/pedal work.
  • Retrograde pedal / distal tibial ("pedal access") — for BTK CTOs that fail antegrade crossing; small sheaths (4Fr or micropuncture), used in SAFARI (below).

Balloon angioplasty principles

  • Sizing: balloon diameter ≈ 1:1 with the reference (healthy) vessel diameter either side of the lesion — undersizing under-treats, oversizing risks dissection/rupture.
  • Length: cover the lesion with a few mm of healthy margin on each side.
  • Pressure: inflate to nominal pressure first; rated burst pressure (RBP) is the ceiling, not the target — heavily calcified lesions may need pressures approaching RBP.
  • Inflation time: typically 60–180 seconds; longer, lower-pressure inflations reduce dissection risk in fragile vessels.
  • POBA (plain old balloon angioplasty) vs DCB (drug-coated balloon, anti-restenotic paclitaxel/sirolimus coating) vs scoring/cutting balloons (micro-blades or wires score the plaque to allow lower-pressure dilation in resistant/calcified lesions).

When angioplasty vs stent vs bypass

The TASC II classification (Trans-Atlantic Inter-Society Consensus) stratifies aorto-iliac and femoro-popliteal lesions A–D by length/complexity: TASC A/B favour an endovascular-first approach; TASC C/D historically favoured surgery, though endovascular-first practice has expanded into C/D with better technology. Stenting is typically reserved for suboptimal PTA result (residual stenosis, flow-limiting dissection) or as primary therapy in the iliacs.

CTO-crossing strategies (overview)

  • Antegrade intraluminal: wire tracks within the true lumen through the occlusion — ideal but not always achievable.
  • Subintimal angioplasty (loop technique): wire deliberately enters the subintimal plane proximal to the occlusion, loops through it, and re-enters the true lumen distally.
  • Retrograde access: a second access point distal to the occlusion (popliteal, tibial, pedal) allows crossing "from the other side" when antegrade fails.
  • SAFARI (Subintimal Arterial Flossing with Antegrade-Retrograde Intervention): combines antegrade + retrograde wires, externalising one wire through the other's sheath to "floss" across a resistant CTO — see Surgical Relevance tab for steps.
⚠️ Exam Trap

Don't confuse balloon nominal pressure with rated burst pressure (RBP) — inflating to RBP as routine practice risks vessel rupture. Also: TASC classification guides first-line strategy, it does not forbid an endovascular attempt at C/D lesions in a patient unfit for surgery.

⚠️ Exam Trap

Rubicon is not a balloon — it's a Boston Scientific 0.014" support/crossing catheter, used to add pushability and allow wire exchanges while crossing tight or calcified lesions, not to dilate them. Grouping it with balloons is a common mix-up.

Other DCB families you'll see across manufacturers: Lutonix (BD), IN.PACT (Medtronic), Stellarex (Philips). All deliver anti-proliferative drug (usually paclitaxel) to the vessel wall to reduce restenosis versus POBA.

SAFARI technique — the steps

Subintimal Arterial Flossing with Antegrade-Retrograde Intervention

1. Antegrade access + attempt at intraluminal/subintimal crossing fails to re-enter the true lumen distally.
2. Retrograde access obtained distal to the occlusion (popliteal or tibial/pedal puncture).
3. Retrograde wire is advanced up into the subintimal/occlusion space to meet the antegrade wire.
4. The retrograde wire is snared or advanced out through the antegrade sheath ("externalised") — the two access points are now linked by one wire, "flossing" the CTO.
5. This externalised wire provides a very stable through-and-through rail; balloon/stent delivery proceeds from the antegrade side.
6. Retrograde access site is closed once treatment is complete.

Stents — brief orientation

  • Self-expanding (nitinol): flexible, conform to tortuous/mobile segments (SFA, popliteal) — deploy to a pre-set diameter.
  • Balloon-expandable: higher radial force, precise deployment — favoured in iliac and renal arteries where the vessel isn't subject to external compression/flexion.
  • Covered stent-grafts: used for perforation, aneurysmal degeneration, or in-stent restenosis.
  • Drug-eluting stents: e.g. Zilver PTX — anti-restenotic coating on a self-expanding platform, mainly SFA.
Surgical Relevance
Complications, decision points, and how endovascular technique intersects with open surgery.

DissectionContrast staining outside lumen on completion angiogramProlonged low-pressure balloon inflation; bail-out stent if flow-limiting

Peri-procedural anticoagulation

Systemic heparin is standard once the sheath is in for anything beyond a very short diagnostic run — target ACT (activated clotting time) 250–300 seconds for peripheral intervention. Under-anticoagulation risks thrombus formation on wires/catheters; this is a recurring exam and practical safety point.

Perforation and distal embolisation

  • Perforation: contrast extravasation on angiogram — first manoeuvre is prolonged low-pressure balloon tamponade at the perforation site; persistent leak needs a covered stent or surgical repair.
  • Distal embolisation: sudden loss of a previously visible distal vessel/pulse after balloon or stent deployment — aspiration thrombectomy or on-table thrombolysis; have a low threshold to check runoff after every intervention.

Endovascular-first vs hybrid vs open

  • Endovascular-first is now standard for most TASC A/B lesions and increasingly for C/D in patients with significant comorbidity or hostile abdomens.
  • Hybrid procedures combine both in one sitting — e.g. femoral endarterectomy (open) with iliac angioplasty/stent (endovascular) for combined inflow/outflow disease; a common MRCS viva scenario.
  • Convert to open when: unable to cross the lesion, uncontrolled perforation, or acute limb-threatening embolisation not amenable to endovascular retrieval.
Viva pearl

If asked "how would you manage combined SFA occlusion and common femoral artery disease?" — the expected answer is a hybrid approach: open common femoral endarterectomy (poor stent durability across the CFA due to flexion and calcification) plus antegrade SFA angioplasty/stent through the same femoral cutdown.
