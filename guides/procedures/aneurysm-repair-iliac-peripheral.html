Principles of Iliac & Peripheral Aneurysm Repair
The central question for any peripheral arterial aneurysm is not "how do I fix it" but "does the territory it supplies need reconstruction at all" — the answer depends entirely on collateral supply.
The organising principle

Every peripheral aneurysm repair decision comes down to one question: if I ligate this vessel and don't reconstruct it, will the distal territory survive on collaterals alone? For the internal iliac artery, usually yes. For the common or external iliac artery, no — the limb depends on that flow.

Classification

  • True aneurysm: all three arterial wall layers involved, degenerative/atherosclerotic in most cases — iliac aneurysms are usually found alongside an AAA, isolated iliac aneurysms are rare.
  • Pseudoaneurysm: contained rupture through the wall, held only by surrounding tissue/haematoma — typically traumatic, iatrogenic (post-catheterisation), or anastomotic.
  • Mycotic (infected) aneurysm: arterial wall infection causing aneurysmal dilation — from septic emboli (endocarditis), bacteraemia seeding an atherosclerotic/damaged wall, or direct spread from adjacent infection. Classically Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus species. Often saccular, rapidly growing, with perianeurysmal fat stranding on CT and a higher rupture risk than degenerative aneurysms of similar size.

Indications for repair

  • Common iliac artery aneurysm: generally repair at ≥3.0–3.5cm (thresholds vary by guideline/institution), symptomatic, or rapidly growing.
  • Internal iliac (hypogastric) artery aneurysm: generally repair at ≥3.0cm, or smaller if symptomatic — internal iliac aneurysms rupture at smaller diameters than common iliac aneurysms.
  • Mycotic aneurysm — any size, always treat. The combination of infection and a weakened wall makes rupture risk unacceptable regardless of diameter; this is a source-control emergency, not a size-threshold decision.

Mycotic aneurysm — the management framework

Three simultaneous goals

1. Source control — excise the infected aneurysm and radically debride all infected/necrotic tissue; send tissue and fluid for culture.
2. Decide on reconstruction — based on the territory supplied (see below), not on habit. Ligation without reconstruction is the correct answer, not a compromise, when collateral supply is adequate.
3. Prolonged antimicrobial therapy — culture-directed, typically weeks to months, guided by microbiology/infectious disease input; some patients need long-term suppressive therapy if any residual infected material can't be fully excised.

Why the internal iliac artery tolerates ligation

The pelvis has a rich collateral network — the "pelvic collateral circle" — linking the internal iliac territory to three other sources:

  • Lumbar arteries → iliolumbar artery (a branch of the posterior division of the internal iliac).
  • Contralateral internal iliac artery, via presacral/pelvic floor anastomoses.
  • Profunda femoris → cruciate anastomosis around the hip → superior/inferior gluteal arteries.

Because of this, unilateral internal iliac artery ligation/excision without reconstruction is generally well tolerated — the rationale directly applicable to a left internal iliac mycotic aneurysm planned for excision alone.

⚠️ Exam Trap

This tolerance applies to unilateral sacrifice. Bilateral internal iliac artery occlusion (e.g. both internal iliacs covered during bilateral EVAR extension) risks buttock/pelvic claudication, erectile dysfunction, and — rarely — colonic or spinal cord ischaemia if the collateral network is itself compromised (e.g. prior IMA ligation, aortic surgery). Preserve at least one internal iliac artery whenever possible.

The same decision framework (ligate vs reconstruct, in-situ vs extra-anatomic) applies across the iliac and peripheral tree — only the consequence of ligation changes.

Mycotic aneurysm — operative principles checklist

  • Wide surgical debridement of all grossly infected and necrotic tissue — inadequate debridement is the leading cause of recurrent/persistent infection.
  • Multiple intra-operative cultures (tissue and fluid, not just a swab) to direct antibiotic duration and choice.
  • If reconstruction is required, cover the repair with a vascularised tissue flap (omentum, muscle) where feasible to reduce reinfection risk.
  • Involve microbiology/infectious diseases early — duration of therapy and choice of suppressive antibiotics is a multidisciplinary decision, not a fixed protocol.
  • Plan surveillance imaging post-op to confirm resolution and detect recurrence or stump complications.
Surgical Relevance
Exposure, intra-operative decision points, and complications — including what to anticipate for a left internal iliac mycotic aneurysm excision.

Exposure

  • Retroperitoneal approach is standard for iliac artery aneurysms — lateral to the peritoneum, avoiding a transperitoneal route through potentially contaminated/inflamed tissue when infection is present.
  • Identify and protect the ureter before dissecting around the internal iliac artery — it crosses anterior to the vessel near the bifurcation and is a recognised injury risk in this exposure.
  • Control proximal (common iliac) and distal (branches of the internal iliac, or external iliac if working near the bifurcation) before manipulating the aneurysm sac, exactly as for any aneurysm exposure — proximal and distal control before you touch the sac.

Left internal iliac mycotic aneurysm — excision without reconstruction: what to anticipate

Working through the case

Rationale for no reconstruction: unilateral internal iliac sacrifice is well tolerated via the pelvic collateral circle (lumbar/iliolumbar, contralateral internal iliac, profunda femoris–gluteal cruciate anastomosis) — reconstructing a vessel that doesn't need it only adds a second operative field for the infection to seed.

Steps: retroperitoneal exposure → proximal control of the internal iliac origin (and common iliac if needed) → identify and preserve the ureter → control the internal iliac branches distally → excise the aneurysm with a margin of macroscopically uninvolved tissue → send multiple cultures → irrigate the field → consider omental coverage of the ligated stump if anatomically feasible.

Watch for post-operatively: buttock claudication (common, usually self-limiting), rarely erectile dysfunction, and monitor for any signs of pelvic/colonic ischaemia — low threshold to reassess if the patient develops unexpected pelvic pain, though this is uncommon with unilateral, planned ligation in a patient without prior contralateral compromise.

Generalising to common iliac or other peripheral aneurysms

If the same patient instead had a common iliac or other limb-supplying peripheral artery mycotic aneurysm, the plan changes fundamentally: ligation alone is not an option because the limb depends on that flow. You would need to weigh in-situ reconstruction (faster, better patency, but risks reinfecting a contaminated field) against extra-anatomic bypass (avoids the infected field, at the cost of a longer conduit and generally lower long-term patency) — with autologous conduit (vein) preferred over prosthetic material whenever the field is or was infected.

Viva pearl

If asked "why not reconstruct?" for an internal iliac aneurysm, the expected answer isn't "because it's infected" — it's the anatomical argument: rich pelvic collateral supply makes unilateral ligation physiologically safe, so reconstruction would add unnecessary risk (graft infection, longer operation) for no perfusion benefit. Infection is the reason to excise; collateral anatomy is the reason not to reconstruct.
